# Liquidity Reimagined: Why Treasury Automation Is Still Only Half Done

**Treasury teams have spent a decade digitising payments, yet only 39% describe their systems as mostly or fully automated, and that gap is where real-time, borderless and resilient liquidity will be won or lost.**

## A decade of digitisation, and a number that should bother you

Payments and the wider treasury function have gone through a major period of digital transformation over the past decade. That's the comfortable version of the story, and it's how J.P. Morgan opens its 2026 report, *Payments Outlook: Five shifts powering payments*. The less comfortable version sits in a survey the report cites, run by J.P. Morgan with the Americas SAP User Group (ASUG), and it's worth reading slowly.

87% of organisations have some level of automation in treasury and payments infrastructure. Stop there and the picture looks finished. Continue to the next figure and it doesn't: only 39% describe their systems as mostly or fully automated. The same survey reports that, for many organisations (48%), the automation they do have is only partial. The sample is small, with n=107 respondents, so I'd treat the percentages as a snapshot rather than a census. Even so, the direction is hard to miss.

The survey also pins down what "automation" means, which matters because the word gets stretched. It refers to the end-to-end integration of treasury and payment processes, including payables and receivables systems, bank connectivity, and the use of automated reconciliation, exception handling and straight-through processing with minimal manual effort. By that definition, automating one step in a chain doesn't make the chain automated. A treasury team that has bank connectivity but still reconciles by hand sits in the partial group.

And the cost of staying there is stated plainly. According to the same J.P. Morgan and ASUG survey, manual processing is the biggest pain point for payments infrastructure. Not fraud, not cost, not regulation: manual work.

My view is that this finding should shape how founders and technical leads read everything else that follows. Real-time liquidity, borderless liquidity and always-on treasury are all attractive ideas. Each one assumes that funds and data move without someone retyping them. If most organisations are only partway to that assumption, the interesting question isn't which advanced capability to buy next. It's which manual step is still blocking the ones already paid for.

## The backdrop: resilient growth, with friction

J.P. Morgan Global Research expects the global economy to remain resilient in 2026. That's a hedged expectation, and it should be read as one. The same material is direct about the drag: trade tensions and the risk of conflict are impacting growth.

Against that backdrop, AI-led investment is expected to be a core driver for the economy over the next five years. Again, that's an expectation rather than a result. Payments is one area that will see huge benefits from AI-enabled technological advances, though that too is a forward-looking statement and not something the report claims has already happened.

Why mention macro conditions in an article about treasury plumbing? Because they explain the pressure. The economic environment and new technologies are accelerating the move to real-time, borderless and resilient liquidity and transforming working capital management. Uncertain conditions raise the value of knowing where cash is and being able to move it. The technology is arriving at the moment the environment is asking more of it.

The finance function is being transformed by data, AI and increased connectivity as companies strive for efficient, automated and transparent treasury systems. Payment technology is already helping to optimise working capital and create visibility to support informed decision-making across the treasury function. "Already" is the operative word there: this part isn't speculation.

## Three kinds of liquidity

Finance leaders are increasingly focusing on real-time liquidity, borderless liquidity and resilient liquidity, as well as dynamic working capital solutions. It's a useful taxonomy because each strand has its own tools, and the tools are listed rather than left vague. Multicurrency management, VAMs and inter-company payment optimisation sit under borderless liquidity. API connectivity, notional pooling and automated sweeping sit under resilient liquidity. Real-time liquidity is the foundation both of the others lean on, and it deserves the first look.

### Real-time liquidity: one line of sight

Real-time liquidity creates visibility into a company's cash position at any given time, across all operating entities. The mechanism behind that centralised line of sight is unglamorous. It comes from streamlining legal entities and bank account footprints, and from integrating banking portals with either treasury management systems (TMS) or enterprise resource planning (ERP) software via API connectivity.

Read that again as an engineering brief. Two things have to happen: fewer entities and accounts to reconcile, and a programmatic connection between the bank and the system of record. Neither is a product you switch on. The first is an organisational decision, and the second is integration work. That's why the 39% figure matters: the visibility layer depends on the same end-to-end integration that the survey's definition of automation describes.

Automation then does the moving. Through automation, funds can be moved between accounts based on predefined events without manual intervention. Automated sweeping allows treasurers to deploy cash just-in-time and automatically redeploy it where it is needed most, removing friction caused by different time zones and cut-off times. Anyone who has watched a cash sweep stall behind a cut-off in another region will see the appeal.

The same logic applies on the receivables side. Michelle Conklin, Managing Director, Head of Receivables Solutions at J.P. Morgan, states that automation is transforming receivables and payment reconciliation by drastically reducing manual intervention and enabling near real-time posting of payments to invoices. Reconciliation is exactly the kind of exception-heavy, manual-by-default task that keeps organisations in the partial-automation group.

### Borderless liquidity: moving value without moving money

The move towards real-time liquidity opens up the possibility of borderless liquidity, in which treasurers can rapidly facilitate instant payments or intra-group financing in multiple countries or currencies. The report frames this as a possibility that real-time capability opens up, and I'd keep that framing. It isn't automatic.

One mechanism is stated in concrete terms. By replacing physical fund movements with virtual account structures for intercompany trade settlement, finance teams can manage internal cash flows and optimise foreign exchange without moving funds between accounts or locations. The appeal is that the movement becomes a ledger event rather than a transfer, so there's less to go wrong and less to wait for.

The wider payments rails matter too. RTP schemes have traditionally been domestic in focus but are expected to become more sophisticated and interlinked with other countries. Two examples carry the point. India's Unified Payments Interface (UPI), described as the world's largest RTP network, processes more than 640 million transactions a day and will expand to more than 20 countries by March 2029. The EU's SEPA now covers 41 countries and is planning to upgrade its transaction limit of €100,000 to a theoretical maximum of just under €1 billion.

Note the strength of each statement. UPI's daily volume is reported as fact. The SEPA upgrade is a plan. The consequence the report draws from it, that for businesses across Europe large-scale payments that once would have taken hours or days to settle will clear in seconds, is a projection. I'd argue the projection is worth taking seriously for treasury planning, but it isn't yet a result, and nothing in the report says when it arrives.

### Resilient liquidity: the safeguard

When turbulent conditions disrupt cash flow or cause expenses to spike, being able to move funds or collateral quickly from different areas of the business can be a vital safeguard. That's the case for resilience in one sentence. It's about optionality: having cash and collateral reachable when the ordinary plan stops working.

Two structural approaches are described. The first is payment centralisation. Treasury departments could consider it, using a regional treasury centre or an in-house bank to consolidate cash instead of relying on local offices. The report offers this as something to consider, not a prescription, and I'd read it that way.

The second is multicurrency notional pooling, in which the balances of multiple different bank accounts are aggregated to provide a single cash position. The practical gain is stated clearly: notional pooling allows treasuries to use surplus cash from one currency account to offset the deficit in another, limiting the need for complex intercompany loans and FX hedges. It can also ensure that trade settlement and automated cash deployment continues even during temporary currency shortages or mismatches.

Put the three strands side by side and the dependency chain is plain. Single cash position, automated sweeping and API connectivity all need clean integration underneath. Virtual accounts and pooling reduce the movement of money, but they still need the bank and the treasury system to agree on what the balances are.

## Always-on treasury, and what speed costs

Always-on treasury is a concept where funds can be moved around an organisation 24/7, based on programmable automation and solutions such as blockchain-based smart contracts. It's the logical endpoint of the automated sweeping described earlier: if rules can trigger movement on predefined events, nothing in principle requires them to wait for business hours.

Institutional adoption of blockchain technology, including stablecoins and tokenisation, is growing rapidly. Over the next few years, technologies like AI, blockchain and API connectivity will further accelerate digitalisation. Transactions are expected to become even more frictionless, fast and convenient. Those last two statements look ahead, and I'd keep them in that register: the report expects acceleration, it doesn't report it as done.

Here's the part I'd argue deserves more attention than it usually gets. With increased transaction speed comes greater risk. The report says so directly, and it follows from the logic of the whole piece. If money can move at any hour on predefined triggers, a bad instruction or a fraudulent one can move just as fast. The automation that removes manual intervention also removes the pause in which a person might have caught the problem.

Cybercriminals are using AI to power up their fraud attempts. The same technology that is expected to benefit payments is being used against it. In response to rising AI-driven fraud, companies are competing in what the report calls the "contest of relentlessness" with stronger digital ID and tighter counterparty validation. Those two controls are the ones named, and I'd note they're both about establishing who is on the other end of a payment rather than about the speed of the payment itself.

Closed-loop ecosystems illustrate the commercial side of the same tension. They give companies greater control over the payments process and a smoother checkout process for customers, including via AI agents, which will increasingly complete transactions on behalf of shoppers. The next wave of innovation, from agentic commerce to embedded payments, is making payments even more frictionless and personalised. Control and convenience arrive together here, and so, by the logic of the speed point above, does the need for validation.

## What this means for a treasury roadmap

Pulling the report's findings into a practical stance, I'd make three arguments, and I'd defend each from the report rather than from taste.

First, finish what you've started before adding what's fashionable. The survey shows 87% with some automation and only 39% mostly or fully automated, with manual processing named as the biggest pain point. Stablecoins, tokenisation and AI agents are on the horizon, and institutional blockchain adoption is growing rapidly, but a treasury that still reconciles by hand has a nearer problem. The survey's own definition of automation, covering payables and receivables systems, bank connectivity, automated reconciliation, exception handling and straight-through processing, reads like a checklist for where the partial implementations stop.

Second, treat visibility as a precondition. Real-time liquidity depends on streamlined legal entities and bank account footprints and on API integration between banking portals and TMS or ERP software. Those are the steps that make a single cash position possible, and notional pooling, automated sweeping and virtual account structures all work better when the cash position they act on is trustworthy.

Third, budget for the risk side at the same time as the speed side. The report doesn't present fraud controls as an afterthought. It presents them as the response to a measurable shift: cybercriminals using AI, and companies answering with stronger digital ID and tighter counterparty validation. If transaction speed rises, the case for those controls rises with it.

None of this argues against the ambitious end of the roadmap. Real-time payment schemes becoming interlinked, SEPA's planned limit upgrade, UPI's expansion to more than 20 countries by March 2029: these are the kind of developments that could make borderless liquidity practical for more treasuries. They're expected rather than complete, and a roadmap should say so.

## The question the data leaves open

The survey reports where organisations stand today, with 87% automated in some form and 39% mostly or fully so. It doesn't tell us how quickly the middle group, the 48% describing partial automation, will move. Nor does the report say which of the three liquidity strands those organisations will prioritise, or whether faster rails will arrive before the controls that make them safe.

That last point is the one to watch. UPI is slated to reach more than 20 countries by March 2029, and SEPA is planning a ceiling just under €1 billion. If settlement in seconds becomes ordinary for large payments across Europe, treasuries will have to show that digital ID and counterparty validation can keep pace with it. Which arrives first, the faster rails or the tighter controls, is the open question worth tracking.
