**An end-to-end resource estimate for factoring RSA-2048 on superconducting cat qubits puts the machine at about 611,000 cat qubits and 1.25 hours when space is traded for time. Its no-bit-flip assumption corresponds to a bit-flip time of about 100 years; relaxing that to about one hour with an extra code layer raises the estimated cost to about 45,000 cat qubits.**

> **Executive Summary**
>
> - The paper presents an end-to-end resource estimation of Shor's algorithm on superconducting cat qubits, compiling the dominant subroutines down to physical-qubit operations.
> - Two architectures are compared: one based on repetition cat codes and one on LDPC cat codes.
> - RSA-2048, the configuration compiled in full, requires about 23,000 cat qubits and 3.14 days. Trading space for time, it runs in 1.25 hours on about 611,000 cat qubits. FFDLP-512 needs about 6,700 cat qubits and 21 minutes.
> - The headline assumption of no bit-flips corresponds to a bit-flip time of about 100 years. A distance-3 bit-flip code as an extra concatenation layer relaxes it to about one hour, with the authors estimating that the cost of RSA-2048 increases to about 45,000 cat qubits.

## Why Shor's algorithm still sets the benchmark

The paper is "Shor's algorithm compiled on cat qubits: 22 593 qubits for RSA-2048 in 3.14 days" by Axel Pappalardo, Dahmun Goudarzi and eight co-authors, most of them at Alice & Bob in Paris, published in 2026 as IACR Cryptology ePrint Archive paper 2026/2665.

In 1994, Peter Shor introduced a quantum algorithm to factor a large number or compute a discrete logarithm in polynomial time. More than three decades on, it remains the reference workload for fault-tolerant quantum hardware, and the reasons are practical rather than sentimental.

The first is that the problem is simple to state and the results are easy to verify. The second is that no known classical algorithm can factor integers of cryptographic size, for example RSA keys of more than 2048 bits, in a reasonable time. Put those two together and you get a clean test: a successful run of Shor's algorithm at such sizes is strong evidence that the computation was run on a quantum computer. There's little room to argue about whether the answer is right, and little room to claim a classical machine did the work.

The same property that makes the algorithm a good test makes it hard to pass. Due to its required depth, the computation cannot succeed without large-scale quantum error correction. Billions of logical operations need to be executed without errors, several orders of magnitude beyond the thousands of operations that a machine without error correction can execute. That gap is the whole story of the field. Nobody closes it by building slightly better physical qubits; it has to be closed by encoding, and the interesting question is how cheaply.

The discrete logarithm variants matter too. For ECDLP, the discrete logarithm on elliptic curves, the best known classical attack is exponential. Development of the ECDLP algorithm has seen three different paths of optimisation emerge in recent publications. On the factoring side, earlier work by Chevignard et al. and by Gidney reduced the number of logical qubits from several times the size of the key to half of it. The algorithm has not been standing still, and any hardware estimate has to be read against a moving algorithmic target.

## Comparing machines requires agreeing on metrics

Resource estimates are only useful if two of them can be compared, and that requires agreement on what to count. The paper lists the application considered, the number of logical qubits, the number of physical qubits, the total execution time and the number of shots. Each has a definition worth keeping straight.

Logical qubits are qubits having low error rates, resulting from quantum error correction applied to groups of manufacturable physical qubits with higher error rates. The number of shots is the average number of executions of the algorithm before success. Trade-offs between the number of physical qubits and time are generally possible, which is why a single headline figure can mislead; the same algorithm on the same hardware can be quoted at several points on a curve.

Across publications, the two primary metrics used to compare architectures are the number of physical qubits and the total execution time. Those are the two numbers this article keeps returning to, and they're the two the paper's results are expressed in.

The paper also explains why these metrics have improved so quickly. Three rather independent levers enable improvement of the two main metrics and explain the rapid evolution of Shor's algorithm from 1994 to 2026. One of them, the high-level algorithm, consists of reducing the number of logical qubits, the number of costly gates, or improving success probability. Each publication improving Shor's algorithm performance acts on one to three of the levers, defining a triplet: a specific algorithm, a specific hardware platform and a specific QEC implementation. This paper's triplet is Shor's algorithm with adapted adders, superconducting cat qubits, and two cat-code architectures.

## What makes a cat qubit different

The qubit technology considered is the dissipative cat qubit, which belongs to the family of superconducting qubits. A variant, the Kerr-cat qubit, is stabilised by a two-photon drive instead. Transmons are the superconducting modality assumed in Gidney–Ekerå, so the comparison point is clear.

The encoding differs in kind. Transmons encode the state in two energy levels of an anharmonic oscillator. The cat qubit instead encodes the state in superpositions of two coherent states of opposite phase of an electromagnetic mode, the so-called Schrödinger cat states.

The consequence that matters for resource counts is the asymmetry in noise. For cat qubits, the bit-flip rate decreases exponentially with a tunable parameter, the number of photons in the cat state, while the phase-flip rate increases only linearly with this parameter. You can buy a great deal of bit-flip suppression and pay only a modest phase-flip penalty. That bias between the two types of noise enables simpler error correction schemes, such as a repetition code architecture, with a much lower logical-to-physical qubit ratio, as only phase-flips need to be corrected. More advanced error correction codes, such as LDPC codes, further lower the ratio.

This is the argument for the whole exercise. If the hardware hands you a noise channel that's already lopsided, a code designed around only one error type needs far fewer physical qubits per logical qubit than a code built for symmetric noise. The estimate in this paper is a test of how far that argument carries through a complete, compiled workload.

The noise model is deliberately compact. It is summarised with a single parameter, pZ, the phase-flip probability, which is mostly driven by the ratio between κ2 (two-photon dissipation, the effect that stabilises the cat state) and κ1 (one-photon loss, the effect that degrades the cat state). The authors call this a critical hardware feature. Bit flips are assumed to be negligible. Hold on to that last sentence; it carries more weight than it first appears to.

## From algorithm to physical qubits

The estimate is built through layers of abstraction. The highest level is the Algorithm layer, which in this work is a description of Shor's algorithm and its subroutines. Algorithms at this level are described with block diagrams, pseudo-code and mathematical representations of the transformations applied to registers.

Below it sits the Logical layer, the lowest level of abstraction that is still independent of the quantum technology considered, thus enabling hardware-agnostic comparisons between algorithms. At this layer each subroutine is described precisely in terms of logical qubits and logical gates or logical operations. That separation is useful to anyone comparing algorithms: two proposals can be judged on logical cost before anyone argues about which hardware runs them.

The paper then goes further down. The dominant subroutines are compiled to physical-qubit operations on two architectures, one based on repetition cat codes and one based on LDPC cat codes. That compilation is what distinguishes this work from a back-of-envelope scaling. The numbers aren't extrapolated from a logical count with a fixed overhead multiplier; they come from compiled operations.

Several new pieces were needed to get there. The authors design a parity-Cnot gate for cat qubits, extend a multi-target Cnot gate to the LDPC-cat architecture, and adapt an active injection procedure to cat-qubit architectures. They also adapt to cat-qubit architectures the adder from Gidney's 2025 resource estimate for factoring RSA-2048. Finally, they combine their adder compilation and a coarser resource estimate for the phaseup with the factory designs and lookup compilation from the FeMoco resource estimation of Kroell et al.

Notice the reuse. The adder comes from a factoring estimate; the factories and lookup compilation come from a chemistry estimate. Borrowing across applications is a sign that the building blocks of fault-tolerant computation are becoming shared infrastructure rather than bespoke designs. It's also a reminder that the phaseup is treated with a coarser estimate than the adder, so not every component carries equal precision.

## The results: three data points

RSA-2048 is the configuration the authors compile in full, and in that configuration it requires about 23,000 cat qubits and 3.14 days. Trading space for time, it runs in 1.25 hours on about 611,000 cat qubits, a trade-off the paper extrapolates from the compiled configuration. That figure sits on the favourable side of the space-time curve in terms of speed: just over an hour for a key size that no known classical algorithm can factor in a reasonable time.

The second data point is a proposal. FFDLP-512 is defined as an instance of the FFDLP problem for a 512-bit prime group and an 80-bit exponent. The authors propose it as an intermediate benchmark, and it requires about 6,700 cat qubits and 21 minutes. Shor's algorithm for FFDLP is space-efficient, which is consistent with a qubit count that's roughly two orders of magnitude below the RSA-2048 figure.

I think the intermediate benchmark is the most useful idea in the paper for anyone building hardware. A target of 611,000 qubits is a destination; it doesn't tell a team what to demonstrate next year. A benchmark at about 6,700 cat qubits and 21 minutes gives a nearer waypoint that still exercises the same algorithmic family. The paper's argument is that FFDLP-512 uses all the blocks needed for RSA-2048, so it can demonstrate an actual end-to-end execution of Shor's algorithm without shortcuts. It is also candid that the cryptanalysis of this problem can be performed classically, so the benchmark's value is as a milestone for the machine rather than as a break in its own right.

## The 100-year assumption

Now to the sentence worth holding on to. Bit flips are assumed to be negligible, and the paper states what that means in hardware terms: the requirement of no bit-flips corresponds to a bit-flip time of about 100 years.

That's a demanding number, and the authors don't leave it standing. The requirement can be relaxed to a bit-flip time of about one hour by using an additional concatenation layer into a distance-3 bit-flip error-correcting code. Architectures like the Elevator code have already been proposed to provide bit-flip correction, so the idea of adding a layer that handles the residual bit-flip channel has precedent.

The price is estimated, and the paper's wording is hedged accordingly: the authors estimate that the cost of RSA-2048 increases to about 45,000 cat qubits. This is an estimate and shouldn't be read as a compiled result in the way the 23,000 figure is. Even so, the shape of the trade is worth stating plainly. The outer code relaxes the requirement on the bit-flip time by a factor of more than a million, at the cost of about twice as many physical qubits: from about 23,000 to about 45,000, with a runtime of at least 3.14 days.

The 45,000 figure should not be set against the 611,000 one. The larger number is the time-efficient end of the space-time curve; the smaller is the compiled configuration with an extra layer of bit-flip protection, so it is an increase over 23,000, not a reduction of 611,000. What can be said is that the 100-year requirement is a design assumption rather than a law of nature, and that the authors identify a concatenation route around it.

## Reading the result as a founder or engineer

For technical readers, the practical lesson is that the estimate rewards looking at which assumptions carry the weight. Three stand out.

The first is the noise bias. Everything about the low logical-to-physical ratio rests on bit-flips being suppressed exponentially in photon number while phase-flips grow only linearly. If a platform can't deliver that asymmetry, the repetition-code and LDPC-code advantages described here don't follow.

The second is the ratio of κ2 to κ1. The paper calls it a critical hardware feature, and since the noise model reduces to the single parameter pZ driven mostly by that ratio, it's the number to watch in any cat-qubit hardware roadmap. A team that can report pZ under realistic operating conditions is giving you the input that this estimate is most sensitive to, at least as far as the paper's own noise model goes.

The third is the bit-flip time, which we've covered. Together these three assumptions define the territory in which the headline numbers hold.

For founders, the useful reading is narrower than a prediction. The paper gives no date by which any of this will be built, and nothing in the paper should be taken as one. What it provides is a calibrated map from hardware properties to resource counts, with a stated two-architecture comparison and an intermediate benchmark. That's the kind of artefact that lets an investor or a hardware team ask a precise question: does your qubit meet the pZ and bit-flip requirements that the estimate assumes?

There's also a point about method. The authors compile the dominant subroutines rather than scaling from logical counts, they reuse components from other published estimates, and they say where they've only produced a coarser figure, as with the phaseup. That openness about which parts are detailed and which are rough is what makes the estimate checkable, and checkability is the property that matters most in a field where headline numbers move by orders of magnitude between papers.

## Where the estimate leaves the field

The rapid evolution of Shor's algorithm from 1994 to 2026 came from three rather independent levers, and this paper pulls on all of them in its own way: algorithmic adaptation of the adder, a specific hardware platform in superconducting cat qubits, and two QEC implementations compiled to physical operations. The result is a set of well-defined figures for RSA-2048: about 23,000 cat qubits and 3.14 days in the compiled configuration, 1.25 hours on about 611,000 cat qubits with space traded for time, and an estimated 45,000 cat qubits if the bit-flip time is only about one hour and an extra concatenation layer handles the bit-flips.

The open question the paper raises is the one sitting between the 23,000 and 45,000 figures. A bit-flip time of about 100 years is a hard target; a bit-flip time of about one hour is a far gentler one, at the cost of a distance-3 code layer whose overhead the authors estimate rather than compile. Whether hardware teams will find it easier to push bit-flip times towards the stricter figure, or to accept the shorter time and pay for the extra layer, is the decision this estimate puts in front of them. And a cleaner way to find out than debating RSA-2048 is already on the table: build toward FFDLP-512, at about 6,700 cat qubits and 21 minutes, and see which of the two routes gets there first.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How many cat qubits does the paper estimate for factoring RSA-2048?**
In the configuration compiled in full, about 23,000 cat qubits (22,593) and 3.14 days. Trading space for time, RSA-2048 runs in 1.25 hours on about 611,000 cat qubits. These figures assume a 1 µs error-correction cycle, a 10 µs reaction time, phase-flip noise with pZ = 5 × 10⁻⁴ and no bit-flips.

**What does the 45,000-qubit figure mean?**
It is the authors' estimate for RSA-2048 when the bit-flip time is only about one hour, so an additional distance-3 bit-flip code layer is needed. It is an increase over the roughly 23,000-qubit configuration, with a runtime of at least 3.14 days, not a reduction of the 611,000-qubit figure.

**What is FFDLP-512 and why propose it?**
FFDLP-512 is an instance of the finite-field discrete logarithm problem for a 512-bit prime group and an 80-bit exponent. The paper proposes it as an intermediate benchmark that uses all the blocks needed for RSA-2048, at about 6,700 cat qubits and 21 minutes. Its cryptanalysis can be performed classically, so it is a hardware milestone rather than a break in its own right.

**Why do cat qubits need fewer physical qubits per logical qubit?**
For cat qubits, the bit-flip rate decreases exponentially with the number of photons in the cat state, while the phase-flip rate increases only linearly. That bias means only phase-flips need to be corrected, which allows a repetition code architecture with a much lower logical-to-physical qubit ratio. LDPC cat codes lower the ratio further.

**Which hardware parameter does the paper call critical?**
The noise model is summarised by a single parameter, pZ, the phase-flip probability, which is mostly driven by the ratio between two-photon dissipation (κ2) and one-photon loss (κ1). The paper calls this a critical hardware feature. The bit-flip time is the other assumption that carries the headline numbers.
