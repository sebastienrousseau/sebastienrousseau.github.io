# Celestial Holography: Symmetry Turns Soft Physics into a Dual Theory

**Celestial holography proposes that the gravitational S-matrix in flat space is a correlator in a two-dimensional conformal field theory, and the strongest case for taking that proposal seriously is that symmetries, soft theorems and observable memory effects were already telling the same story before anyone wrote the duality down.**

## Why a flat-space holography needs a new kind of dual

Holography is most familiar as a statement about spacetimes with a negative cosmological constant. Celestial holography goes after a different target. It seeks to establish a holographic correspondence as a new approach to quantum gravity in asymptotically flat spacetimes. Those are a class of solutions to Einstein's equations with vanishing cosmological constant and localised matter stress-energy.

The proposal itself is compact. It posits a duality between the gravitational S-matrix and correlators in a conformal field theory living on the celestial sphere. The wording matters: this is a proposed duality, and the framework should be read as a programme under construction rather than a settled equivalence. This article draws on "Celestial Holography" by Sabrina Pasterski, Monica Pate and Ana-Maria Raclariu (arXiv:2111.11392, 2021), a white paper solicited for the 2022 Snowmass process. It reviews the motivation from asymptotic symmetries, the fundamentals of the proposed holographic dictionary, potential applications to experiment and theory, and some important open questions. Throughout, it restricts to massless scattering in 3+1 dimensions, so every statement below carries that limit with it.

What makes the proposal more than a guess is where it comes from. The route runs through symmetry, and it's worth walking through in order, because each step is forced by the one before.

## Asymptotic symmetries: the residue left after gauge fixing

Asymptotic symmetries are diffeomorphisms that preserve the causal structure of the boundary in a conformal compactification. Put differently, they can be identified as the residual diffeomorphisms that remain after fixing appropriate gauge and boundary conditions. They're what survives when you've removed the redundancy and still have transformations that act non-trivially at the boundary.

The interesting part is how far the boundary conditions are allowed to stretch. Studies that relaxed them enough to permit local enhancements of the Lorentz subgroup, called superrotations, appeared only rather recently. That relaxation is not a technical footnote. It's the hinge on which the rest of the story turns, because a local enhancement of Lorentz symmetry is exactly the kind of structure a two-dimensional conformal field theory knows how to host.

The symmetry generators, the white paper notes, are naturally represented by currents in a two-dimensional theory. A four-dimensional gravitational problem keeps producing two-dimensional objects. That recurrence is the first hint that the dual lives on the celestial sphere.

## One structure, three faces: memory, soft theorems and Ward identities

There is an equivalence between memory effects, soft theorems and Ward identities for asymptotic symmetries. This is the core observation of the soft physics programme, and it's demonstrated rather than conjectured. The Ward identities for asymptotic symmetries manifest as soft theorems of the S-matrix. The memory effect is a directly observable consequence of asymptotic symmetries; more broadly, memory effects are low-frequency observable signatures of symmetry enhancements.

The equivalence is useful in both directions. An amplitude-level statement about soft particles is also a statement about a symmetry, and a symmetry statement is also a prediction about what a detector could in principle see. A theorist working with S-matrix elements and an experimentalist thinking about low-frequency signals are, on this account, discussing the same object.

The superrotation story shows the equivalence doing real work. Speculation about the physicality of superrotations led to the discovery of a subleading soft graviton theorem. That theorem in turn established the Ward identity for a Virasoro symmetry. A speculative idea about a symmetry produced a concrete theorem about scattering, and the theorem confirmed the symmetry's Ward identity. It's rare for a conceptual conjecture to pay off that cleanly.

From there the argument for the dual follows directly. A holographic dual to quantum gravity in four-dimensional asymptotically flat spacetimes must admit a two-dimensional conformal symmetry. The proposed dual theory built upon this symmetry is called the celestial conformal field theory, or CCFT.

## Building the dictionary: Mellin transforms and boost eigenstates

A dual theory is only useful if there's a way to translate between the two sides. The first entry is the celestial amplitude. Celestial amplitudes recast the gravitational S-matrix into a basis of boost eigenstates, and they transform like the correlation functions of a conformal field theory. That gives a key entry in the holographic dictionary.

The mechanics are specific. For massless particles, the transformation from momentum to boost eigenstates can be achieved via a Mellin transform in the energy. The reason the result behaves like a CFT object is that Lorentz transformations in four dimensions are equivalent to global conformal transformations on the celestial sphere. This guarantees that operators constructed from Mellin-transformed wavepackets transform as quasi-primaries with conformal weights (h, h̄).

Gravity then adds something. In a gravitational theory, these quasi-primaries are enhanced to Virasoro primaries via their coupling to the subleading soft graviton. The subleading soft graviton mode couples to an external particle of helicity ℓ and energy ω through the operators ĥ and h̄ˆ. So the Virasoro structure isn't imposed from outside; it falls out of how the soft graviton attaches to ordinary external states.

Finite-energy scattering states, characterised by normalisable single-particle wavepackets in the bulk, are captured by celestial operators with spectrum ∆ = 1 + iλ, λ ∈ R. This is a concrete statement about which conformal dimensions correspond to physical, normalisable states. It tells you which part of the complex ∆ plane carries ordinary scattering.

## What the soft sector looks like from the celestial side

Once soft theorems are translated into the boost eigenstate basis, they acquire a clean representation-theoretic reading. The conformal dimensions of operators in soft theorems admit shortened representations of the global conformal symmetry. That explains the observed organisation of soft theorems into multiplets. A pattern that could look like a bookkeeping accident on the S-matrix side is, on the celestial side, a consequence of representation theory.

A related result concerns factorisation. Factorisation in the soft limit of scattering persists in the boost eigenstate basis. The change of basis doesn't destroy a structural property that physicists rely on, which is the sort of check a proposed dictionary has to pass.

Then there are the poles. As a function of the net conformal dimension, celestial amplitudes admit a series of simple poles. Their locations distinguish UV completions of quantum field theories from expected UV completions of quantum gravity, and the residues reproduce the coefficients in an EFT expansion. For a technical reader this is where the framework starts to feel like a tool. The analytic structure of a single object separates two classes of ultraviolet behaviour and recovers effective-field-theory data.

## The celestial CFT is not an ordinary CFT

If the CCFT were a standard conformal field theory, one could import the usual machinery and be done. The white paper's picture is more interesting: some pieces fit the standard toolkit, and others don't.

Superrotations establish a Virasoro symmetry that is naturally incorporated into the standard CFT technology. That part is familiar. But global translations, as well as the infinity of soft symmetries associated with (half)-integer dimension insertions, imply non-standard constraints on the CFT data. The theory has symmetry requirements that go beyond what a textbook CFT supplies, and those requirements carry bulk information.

The OPE data of the celestial CFT encode the behaviour of scattering in collinear limits. That ties a piece of two-dimensional CFT structure to a recognisable physical regime in four dimensions. The Ward identities arising from soft theorems then lead to recursion relations for the OPE data.

Here the paper's statements become suggestive, and the strength of each statement should be read carefully. Given certain analyticity assumptions in ∆, the Ward identities are sufficient to completely bootstrap the leading and, in some cases, subleading collinear behaviour of scattering. The qualifier is doing real work: the result is conditional on analyticity assumptions, and it covers subleading behaviour only in some cases.

The white paper draws a broader inference, and it too is hedged. This feature of CCFT apparently allows four-dimensional kinematics to determine the dynamics of the two-dimensional dual. There is also recent evidence that celestial symmetries can be used to determine certain bulk dynamics. I'd argue this is the most consequential idea in the framework, provided it holds up. In most quantum field theories, symmetry constrains dynamics but doesn't fix it. The paper calls this a quite unique feature of CCFT, and its own word "apparently" marks the claim as hedged rather than shown.

## From formalism to detectors: the memory effects

A holographic proposal that never touched observation would be hard to defend to a sceptical experimentalist. Celestial holography has a more concrete connection than most, because memory effects are low-frequency observable signatures of symmetry enhancements.

The soft physics programme has identified new memory effects in both gravity and gauge theory: spin memory in gravity, and colour memory in gauge theory. For the lesser-known memories, there are various proof-of-concept measurement proposals and experimental feasibility studies.

The leading gravitational memory effect is further along. Experimental investigations into it are already underway. And there's a practical lesson from gravitational-wave analysis: the choice of BMS frame plays an important role in extracting the correct gravitational waveform. A formal property of the asymptotic symmetry group shows up as a concrete data-analysis consideration, since an incorrect frame means an incorrect waveform.

Colour memory has its own natural home. It is a natural observable in the physical regime that will be probed by the recently launched electron-ion collider (EIC) project. The paper stops short of promising results and poses two direct questions instead. Can spin memory be measured by LISA? Will color memory be observed at the EIC? Both remain open. Anyone presenting either as an expected detection would be going beyond the evidence.

## Infrared divergences and soft hair

Soft physics also reaches a problem that has long irritated anyone computing scattering amplitudes: infrared divergences. The symmetry perspective offers an explanation in terms of charge conservation.

Asymptotic charge non-conservation provides an interpretation of the vanishing amplitude for emitting a finite number of soft particles. Include an infinite number of soft emissions, and one obtains a finite result precisely because the contributions from the soft quanta render the asymptotic charges conserved. The vanishing and the finiteness are two sides of one accounting rule.

The consequence is a general principle. By identifying symmetry as the underlying origin of infrared divergences, this perspective establishes a general principle for constructing infrared safe amplitudes. For practitioners, that converts what is often handled as a technical nuisance into something with a structural cause.

The same family of ideas extends to black holes and other horizons, which can carry soft hair. The paper describes determining the precise phrasing of these statements within the CCFT framework as an active area of research, so this is a pointer to further work rather than a developed result.

## Where the argument is strong and where it isn't

It helps to separate what is demonstrated from what is proposed. Several things stand on firm ground in the white paper's account. The equivalence between memory effects, soft theorems and Ward identities is demonstrated. So is the route from superrotation speculation to the subleading soft graviton theorem and the Virasoro Ward identity. Celestial amplitudes transform like CFT correlators. The simple-pole structure and the reading of residues as EFT coefficients are stated as results, and so are the recursion relations for OPE data.

Other parts are explicitly softer. The duality itself is proposed. The claim that analyticity assumptions let the Ward identities bootstrap collinear behaviour is conditional. The suggestion that four-dimensional kinematics determines two-dimensional dynamics is hedged with "apparently", and the evidence that celestial symmetries fix certain bulk dynamics is described as recent evidence, not as proof. The experimental questions about LISA and the EIC are open.

My own view is that this division is a strength. A framework whose firmest results sit at the level of symmetry, soft theorems and analytic structure, and whose boldest claims are flagged as boldest, is one that can be tested. The firm results are checkable against known soft theorems. The bold ones tell you where to push.

Two limits also apply to everything above. The scope is massless scattering in 3+1 dimensions, so nothing here speaks to other dimensions or to massive particles. And the dictionary is described as proposed, with the celestial CFT defined by its symmetry structure rather than by a full independent construction.

## What the open questions point to

The most pressing technical question is how far the bootstrap idea extends. The Ward identities lead to recursion relations for OPE data, and under analyticity assumptions in ∆ they are sufficient to completely bootstrap the leading, and in some cases subleading, collinear behaviour. The natural next question is which cases remain beyond reach, and whether the analyticity assumptions can be justified rather than assumed. If they can, the suggestion that kinematics fixes dynamics gains real weight. If they can't, the scope of the bootstrap narrows to what the assumptions allow.

The experimental side offers the other test. Proof-of-concept measurement proposals and feasibility studies already exist for spin and colour memory, and investigations into the leading gravitational memory effect are underway. Whether spin memory can be measured by LISA, and whether colour memory will be observed at the EIC, will show how directly the symmetry structure underlying celestial holography reaches into instruments. Those answers are not in hand, and nothing in the framework guarantees them. Which of the two questions resolves first is itself worth watching.
