## The Cohn-Elkies program and what it bounds

Sphere packing asks how densely non-overlapping balls can fill space. The document fixes notation for this. For centres separated by at least 1, ∆d is the supremum of the upper densities of radius-1/2 balls in Rd. Put differently, ∆d denotes the maximal sphere-packing density in Rd. Everything in the collection's first chapter is measured against that quantity.

The Cohn-Elkies program gives an upper bound on ∆d. Its optimal output is written LPd, defined as the optimal density bound furnished by the program. The mechanism is the Gorbachev–Cohn-Elkies bound, which assigns every f in a class called Ad the density upper bound vd f(0)/(2d f̂(0)). In words, you pick an auxiliary function f from the admissible class, compute its value at the origin and the value of its Fourier transform at the origin, form that ratio with the volume and scaling factors, and the result bounds the packing density from above. The linear program searches for the f that makes this bound as small as possible.

This is a framework in which the whole difficulty is in choosing f well. A cleverly chosen function gives a tight bound, and a poorly chosen one gives a weak bound that is still valid. That's why the matching lower bound in the new result matters so much. It speaks about every admissible function at once, and it turns a search for better functions into a closed question.

## What the framework had already achieved

Before turning to the new theorem, it's worth being specific about what the Fourier-analytic linear-programming framework had already delivered, because the new result is partly a statement about how much further it can go.

## The sign-uncertainty problem

When no such radius exists, r(g) = ∞. The uncertainty problem concerns how small this radius can be made for functions with a prescribed relationship to their own Fourier transforms.

That relationship is a sign. The condition ĝ = ςg ties a function to its Fourier transform up to the sign. The signs +1 and −1 give the original and complementary uncertainty problems, respectively.
