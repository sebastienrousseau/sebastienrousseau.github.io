# What the FCA Expects Before You Launch an AI Agent in UK Finance

**The FCA's statement on agentic AI keeps responsibility with regulated firms and their senior managers, and tells technology providers that the label they give themselves does not decide whether they need authorisation.**

> **Executive Summary**
>
> - The FCA says firms across financial services are moving from experimentation to deployment of AI agents, and that it is engaging with firms looking to offer personal AI agent products to UK consumers.
> - Its regulatory framework is principles-based and outcomes-focused, and it applies when regulated firms use AI.
> - Responsibility for compliance remains with a regulated firm and its senior managers, including where the firm relies on a third-party provider.
> - Technology providers and social media platforms considering an agentic product in the UK need to consider whether they are carrying out FCA-regulated activities.
> - The FCA expects AI providers to back clear disclaimers with effective operational safeguards, and it intends to launch an Agentic Policy Sprint.

## What the FCA published, and why now

The FCA's page "Our approach to Agentic AI" sets out its expectations for firms, technology providers and social media platforms looking to develop and deploy agentic AI products in the financial services sector. It follows a statement the FCA published with other regulators in the Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum (DRCF) on firms developing and deploying personal AI agents. The new page is more specific to financial services, and the FCA says it will provide regular updates on it to support the development and roll out of agentic products at pace.

The update dated 24 November 2026 opens with an observation: across financial services, firms are moving from experimentation to deployment of AI agents, and the FCA is engaging with firms looking to offer personal AI agent products to UK consumers.

The statement opens with the upside. The FCA says that, deployed well, AI can increase productivity, improve competition and services, and drive economic growth, and that AI agents can widen access to financial tools and information and be part of new products that offer significant consumer benefit and convenience. The concern it states is that these products can also blur the boundary between regulated and unregulated activity, and the protections consumers receive in relation to their finances and data.

The core expectation sits in one sentence. As AI becomes capable of moving beyond providing information to initiating transactions, moving money, arranging borrowing or making investment decisions, all at speed and scale, firms must understand which activities require authorisation and where regulatory requirements may apply. The same passage adds a point that is easy to skip: firms must be mindful of the operational resilience of their platforms and their commercial partners.

The FCA also says some products in this space may require FCA regulation at launch, or evolve in a way that makes regulation apply, and that it will assess the services firms provide through agentic products, including how they develop over time. My reading is that classification is therefore not a one-off exercise at launch: a product that adds capabilities may need to revisit it.

## Regulated firms: responsibility does not move with the model

For regulated firms, the central sentence is about responsibility. If a regulated firm develops or deploys agentic AI, or relies on a third-party provider, responsibility for complying with regulatory obligations remains with the firm and the senior managers within it. The FCA describes its framework as principles-based and outcomes-focused, and says it applies when regulated firms use AI.

The statement then says what the FCA expects firms to be able to show. Firms should demonstrate that they understand the systems they use, the degree of autonomy those systems have, the risks they create, and the controls necessary to manage those risks in real time and in stress situations.

Each part of that sentence is something a firm can be asked about. The degree of autonomy is a design property: how much an agent can do on its own. The phrase "in real time and in stress situations" is the one I would dwell on as an engineer. The statement does not prescribe any particular control, but a control that only works in calm conditions, or only after the event, does not obviously answer it.

The FCA points firms to its publication on AI good practice and areas for improvement, which sets out areas to consider and practical examples of how firms have done this for AI use cases.

## Senior managers and the Consumer Duty

The statement is specific about senior managers. They are responsible for any AI models deployed within the area of the business they are responsible for, whether developed in house or by a third party. They need to take reasonable steps to understand the risks arising from AI, provide effective oversight, challenge deployment, and make sure governance, controls and escalation arrangements are appropriate for the risks.

"Challenge deployment" is the phrase I would underline. Receiving a briefing is not the same as being able to question a proposed deployment and, where the risks warrant it, push back.

For founders selling agentic products into regulated firms, my inference, not the FCA's, is a commercial one: the buyer's senior managers have to be able to understand and challenge what they are buying. A product whose autonomy, risks and controls cannot be explained to them will be harder for them to take responsibility for.

The Consumer Duty applies alongside this. The FCA notes that under the Duty, firms' obligations include avoiding causing foreseeable harm, ensuring that consumers get the information they need, and helping support customers to pursue their financial objectives. The Duty also contains specific provisions on governance and culture, monitoring consumer outcomes, and redress or other appropriate action. Read next to an agentic deployment, that suggests to me that a firm will need to see what its agent is doing to customer outcomes, and have a way to put things right.

## When customers arrive with their own agents

The statement also covers a case that is easy to miss: customers who use AI agents to interact with a firm. The FCA says firms should consider how they will respond, including:

- how they establish that they are dealing with an agent;
- what consents are in place;
- what an AI agent is allowed to do;
- how customers can get support if something goes wrong.

The FCA adds that this includes information provided to consumers through agentic chatbots, or where agents are used to help resolve complaints.

Payments get their own sentence. Firms should consider their systems and controls, any liabilities that may arise from accepting instructions, and whether it is appropriate to accept payments instructed by an AI agent, or from a digital wallet controlled by an AI agent. The FCA does not tell firms to accept or to refuse. It asks them to consider it. In my view each of those questions is as much an engineering problem as a policy one: recognising an agent, recording what the customer authorised it to do, and keeping a support route open for the person behind it.

## Technology providers: the label does not set the perimeter

The section addressed to technology providers and social media platforms is direct. If you are considering launching an agentic product in the UK, you need to consider whether you are carrying out activities regulated by the FCA, and the FCA asks you to speak to it.

Technology providers, it says, should consider carefully the activities their agents actually undertake. Just because a business describes itself as a technology provider does not mean that it does not need to be authorised by the FCA to provide financial services in the UK. The statement adds that this boundary is set by Parliament.

The FCA gives examples of what to think about, which could include whether you are:

- offering payments services;
- issuing financial promotions;
- providing an authorised information service;
- engaging in credit broking or intermediation;
- facilitating or offering financial advice.

These are framed as examples ("this could, for example, include"), not as a complete list or a verdict on any product. The statement also says you need to think about whether other regulatory requirements apply, not only regulated activities.

The FCA acknowledges that these questions can be complex, and says that personalised context, which some large language models rely on, can amplify this complexity. It does not explain the mechanism further, and I will not supply one.

## Disclaimers, safeguards and intervention powers

The FCA expects AI providers to help consumers understand the limits of financial information and advice generated by their models, through clear, proportionate and consistent disclaimers, and to bolster these with effective operational safeguards. The second half is what I would hold a product team to. A disclaimer on its own is not what the expectation describes.

The statement also says the FCA has powers to intervene if it believes regulated financial services are being offered without its authorisation, and that it will not hesitate to use them if necessary. It explains why the powers exist: to provide appropriate safeguards against scams and financial loss, and to ensure consumers have effective support and redress when things go wrong.

The engagement language and the intervention language sit side by side in the same statement. I would not read the first as softening the second.

## How to engage, and what remains open

The FCA and other regulators say they welcome early engagement and provide services to help innovators understand which regulatory requirements may apply and to test products. The statement names several routes:

- the AI Lab, which provides a range of services to support firms developing and testing new agentic services and products;
- the FCA's innovation services;
- the pre-application support service;
- a dedicated Agentic Policy Sprint, which the FCA intends to launch for firms, policymakers and tech providers to collaborate, with expressions of interest registered by email to AgenticAI@fca.org.uk.

The Sprint is an intention, not a delivered programme. The statement does not say when it will run, what it will cover, or what it will produce.

Several questions stay open. The statement does not say what controls "in real time and in stress situations" look like in practice. It does not resolve where information ends and regulated activity begins when a model relies on personalised context; it acknowledges that this adds complexity. And it promises regular updates, so the page itself is likely to change. The question I would watch is whether those updates, and the Sprint, turn the statement's careful "consider" language into firmer guidance on where the perimeter sits for agents.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What did the FCA publish on agentic AI?**
On 24 November 2026 the FCA published "Our approach to Agentic AI", setting out its expectations for firms, technology providers and social media platforms looking to develop and deploy agentic AI products in the financial services sector. It builds on a statement published with regulators in the Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum, and the FCA says it will update the page regularly.

**Does using a third-party AI provider move regulatory responsibility to the provider?**
No. The FCA says that if a regulated firm develops or deploys agentic AI or relies on a third-party provider, responsibility for complying with regulatory obligations remains with the firm and its senior managers. Senior managers are responsible for AI models deployed in their area, whether developed in house or by a third party.

**Can a technology company avoid FCA authorisation by calling itself a technology provider?**
No. The statement says that a business describing itself as a technology provider does not mean it does not need to be authorised to provide financial services in the UK, and that this boundary is set by Parliament. Providers should consider the activities their agents actually undertake.

**What should firms consider when customers use AI agents to deal with them?**
The FCA lists how to establish that the firm is dealing with an agent, what consents are in place, what the agent is allowed to do and how customers can get support. Firms should also consider their systems and controls, liabilities from accepting instructions, and whether to accept payments instructed by an AI agent or from a wallet an agent controls.

**How can firms engage with the FCA on agentic products?**
Through the AI Lab, the FCA's innovation services and its pre-application support service. The FCA also intends to launch an Agentic Policy Sprint for firms, policymakers and tech providers, with expressions of interest registered by email to AgenticAI@fca.org.uk.
