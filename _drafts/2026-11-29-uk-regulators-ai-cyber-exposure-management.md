**The joint PRA, FCA and Bank of England publication of 19 November 2026 argues that AI is cutting the cost, skills and time threat actors need to discover and exploit vulnerabilities, so firms need to evolve from traditional vulnerability management towards continuous exposure management and learn to act at machine speed.**

> **Executive Summary**
>
> - The publication is issued jointly by the PRA, the FCA and the Bank of England (collectively, the regulators) and highlights effective practices for managing cyber resilience and risks relating to AI.
> - The practices are complementary to, but separate from, the regulators' supervisory statement and Final Guidance (SS/FG) on cyber risk and resilience management, and are not part of the regulators' formal expectations.
> - Recent AI developments are having a material impact on cyber risk by significantly reducing the cost, skills and time threat actors need to discover and exploit vulnerabilities.
> - Firms need to evolve towards continuous exposure management; the key challenges are to prioritise, validate, remediate and recover from threats at machine speed.
> - Effective practice for boards and senior management is to ensure appropriate cyber controls (design) and to obtain assurance that those controls will perform during incidents (effectiveness).
> - AI-related cyber risks could threaten financial stability if they affect shared resources used by many firms.

## What the regulators published, and what status it has

The document is a joint publication from the Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA), the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) and the Bank of England, which it calls collectively the regulators. It was published on 19 November 2026, and its stated purpose is to highlight effective practices for managing cyber resilience and risks relating to artificial intelligence for firms and financial market infrastructures. The term "firm" is used in the same way as in the regulators' supervisory statement and Final Guidance on cyber risk and resilience management, and it includes financial market infrastructures.

Its status is worth getting right, because it's easy to misread. The effective practices are complementary to, but separate from, that SS/FG, which a footnote says is now subject to consultation. They provide more detail on areas relevant to AI, but they're not part of the regulators' formal expectations. The expectations in the SS/FG, by contrast, apply regardless of the technology firms use. So there are two layers: a body of expectations that doesn't change with the technology, and a set of practices that describes what good looks like when AI is in the picture.

That distinction shapes how a founder or security lead should read the document. It isn't a new rulebook. It's a statement of effective practice that builds on relevant publications by the regulators, HM Treasury, the National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC), non-UK authorities, international standard-setting bodies and industry, and that has been informed by the regulators' engagement with firms. A document assembled from that range of sources is worth reading as a signal of direction, even though it sits outside the formal expectations.

The practices are meant to be used proportionately. Firms can adopt them in a manner appropriate to their size, complexity and risk profile. They're also described as particularly relevant for AI systems whose autonomy and capabilities create high cybersecurity risks and, in many cases, opportunities to strengthen firms' cyber defences.

## A deliberate choice about terminology

The publication takes a careful line on vocabulary. It uses the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) definition of an AI system. It also avoids terms such as "frontier AI", which can change meaning or become ambiguous as technology evolves.

This is a small detail with a practical consequence. A practice anchored to a named capability tier would need rewriting each time the tier's meaning drifted. By tying itself to a stable definition and to properties of the systems themselves, the document sidesteps that problem. In its proportionality paragraph it names three such properties: an AI system's autonomy, its level of access to a firm's assets, and its ability to interact with the external environment. Firms may consider those when weighing the risks of AI systems they rely on, alongside their reliance on AI for important business services and critical operations. Both are framed as things firms may consider, not obligations.

## The threat picture: cost, skills and time

The core diagnosis is stated plainly. Recent developments in AI are having a material impact on firms' cyber risk and resilience by significantly reducing the cost, skills and time required for threat actors to discover and exploit vulnerabilities.

The mechanism is specific. Advanced AI systems can analyse source code, identify vulnerabilities and discover attack paths at great speed, allowing threat actors to exploit vulnerabilities within hours or days rather than weeks or months. If you run a patching programme on a cycle measured in weeks, that sentence should make you uncomfortable.

The publication also draws on the UK AI Security Institute (AISI). It's cited as noting that AI capabilities are advancing rapidly across all tested domains, that performance in some areas is doubling every eight months, and that expert baselines are being surpassed rapidly. It also notes that safeguards are improving but vulnerabilities remain, and that some capabilities required for AI models to evade human control are emerging. A further point concerns the open and closed source divide: the performance gap between open and closed source models has narrowed over the past two years. I'd read that last observation carefully. It's reported as a trend, and the document doesn't extend it into a claim about who has access to what.

One counterweight keeps the picture honest. While AI is increasing the scale and speed of vulnerability discovery and exploitation and reducing technical barriers to entry, humans remain central to advanced cyber threat activity. The document does plan for cyber-attacks by highly autonomous AI agents, but its summary describes people with cheaper, faster tools rather than people being replaced.

The publication adds that firms' traditional cyber defences may struggle to cope with this new landscape. That's hedged, and it should be read as a risk the regulators see rather than a verdict on any particular defence.

## Why the bottleneck has moved

The most consequential sentence in the document is arguably its statement about where firms need to head. Because of the increase in the number of vulnerabilities being discovered and the speed at which they're being identified, firms need to evolve from traditional vulnerability management, where discovery has historically been the main bottleneck, towards continuous exposure management.

Think about what that implies. For years, a security team's scarce resource was finding the flaw. Scanning, penetration testing and researcher disclosure all existed because discovery was slow and expensive. If discovery gets cheaper and faster, the scarcity sits elsewhere: in what the firm does after a weakness exists, and in how quickly.

The publication names the resulting challenges directly: to prioritise, validate, remediate and recover from threats at machine speed. Each verb is a separate capability. Prioritising means deciding which of many findings matters. Validating means confirming that a finding is real. Remediating means fixing it. Recovering means restoring service when prevention fails. The phrase "at machine speed" applies to all four.

My view is that this framing is right, and that it's more demanding than it first appears. A firm that bolts an AI scanner onto an unchanged process will find more issues and fix them no faster. The document anticipates this. It says scaling the use of advanced AI systems depends as much on people, processes and remediation capacity as on the AI capability itself, and that firms measuring their tooling should count not only the vulnerabilities identified but the ones disclosed, mitigated or remediated in ways that meaningfully reduced risks.

## Defining exposures

The publication defines exposures as any misconfigurations, security gaps or weaknesses that a threat actor could exploit. That's broader than a catalogue of known software vulnerabilities. A misconfiguration isn't a CVE. A security gap might be a missing control rather than a defective component. By choosing "exposure" as the unit of work, the document widens what a firm has to track.

The first step in managing exposures effectively is to identify them. That involves determining, mapping and understanding which assets a threat actor could exploit, and the connections and dependencies between those assets. The dependencies part matters. An asset that looks harmless alone can be an entry point because of what it connects to.

## Identifying exposures in practice

The publication is concrete about where firms can start. Firms can use existing sources to help identify and document exposures, including AI and model inventories, operational resilience maps and self-assessments, outsourcing and third-party registers, and software bills of materials.

That list is useful precisely because none of it is new. Most regulated firms already maintain several of these artefacts for other reasons. The practical step is to treat them as inputs to a single exposure picture rather than as separate compliance outputs.

There's a caveat, and the document states it. Firms may need to carefully manage gaps, inconsistencies and silos in the data obtained from those sources. An outsourcing register built for one purpose and a software bill of materials built for another won't necessarily agree about what's deployed. The hedge in the source is appropriate; the extent of the problem will vary by firm.

To help, firms can use dynamic asset identification tools to automate parts of the identification process and identify potential blind spots. The point of that phrasing is the blind spots. Static inventories record what someone remembered to write down. Automation can surface what nobody did. The document also suggests firms may prioritise identifying exposures linked to their important business services or critical operations.

## AI systems introduce their own exposure surface

The publication lists where exposures can arise, and several entries are specific to AI: AI systems, including those available with open weights; APIs and other model endpoints; identities, including non-human ones such as AI agents; and external connectors, which it defines as components that link an AI system with external data sources, tools or APIs. Connectors are where an AI system's access to a firm's assets meets the outside world, which is why I read them alongside the autonomy, access and external-interaction properties the document asks firms to weigh.

Dependencies are a second concern. Open-source and open-weight dependencies, some of which may be widely embedded in other software and not immediately visible, are on the same list. The careful reading is that firms may not see these components unless they look for them. Later the document suggests software composition analysis tools can help identify open-source dependencies that may not be immediately visible.

A third issue is behavioural. Advanced AI systems are non-deterministic: they may produce different results across repeated assessments, making validation of their outputs important. That has a direct operational meaning. If you use an AI system to assess exposures, one run isn't necessarily the whole answer. Validation, which already sits in the document's list of machine-speed challenges, is the control that addresses it.

This connects to a broader statement. The publication says it's important not to over-rely on AI, and that appropriate human oversight remains essential, especially for key decisions. Non-determinism is a concrete reason why. A tool that can give different answers on repeated assessments needs someone accountable for checking them.

## Defence and attack aren't symmetrical

AI has both defensive and offensive capabilities, and it would be tempting to assume the two sides benefit equally. The document doesn't. It says the respective capabilities of firms to use AI for cyber defence and of threat actors to use AI for cyber-attacks are not always symmetrical. That's hedged, so "not always" matters.

It gives an example. Effectively managing cyber risks requires firms to secure financial and non-financial resources and adopt appropriate governance. Conversely, threat actors typically just need to focus on identifying and exploiting vulnerabilities while avoiding detection. My reading is that the attacker's task is narrower, and that this narrowness can give the offence an advantage in some circumstances.

For a founder deciding how much to invest in AI-assisted defence, the implication is cautionary rather than reassuring. Having access to the same class of tools as an attacker doesn't mean parity.

## Execution speed

The most operational idea in the document is execution speed. In addition to faster decision-making, effective practice may require firms to increase the speed at which they identify affected assets, test remediation, secure a change window, coordinate with third parties, deploy remediation and verify its success. The source calls this execution speed.

Read that list as a pipeline and the weak links become obvious. Securing a change window and coordinating with third parties are rarely technical problems. They're scheduling and contractual ones. A firm can have an AI system that finds a flaw in minutes and still spend days waiting for an approval slot. That's why execution speed is a useful concept: it measures the whole path from knowing about a weakness to confirming it's closed.

The document links this to a trade-off. In its paragraph on boards and senior management, it says effective practice may involve using AI to strengthen cyber defences and reassessing trade-offs, for example remediating a critical vulnerability quickly even if doing so could disrupt a service. The source hedges this with "may", and the example is deliberate. It asks firms to consider whether protecting service availability by default is still right when threat actors can exploit vulnerabilities within hours or days. The document doesn't say the answer is always to patch immediately. It talks about balancing speed of remediation against service continuity, and about compensating controls, such as restricting access, isolating assets, filtering traffic and virtual patching, where a fix can't be applied straight away.

## Boards, senior management and the nature of cyber risk

The publication states that cyber risk is no longer a purely technical issue. It is a core business risk and leadership responsibility. I think that sentence does more work than it appears to, because it changes who owns the exposure-management problem.

For boards and senior management, effective practice is not only to ensure the firm has appropriate cyber controls, which the document labels design, but also to obtain assurance that those controls will perform appropriately during incidents, which it labels effectiveness. The pairing is the useful part. A control can be well designed and still fail under pressure. Asking only whether a control exists addresses the first half. Asking whether it will perform during an incident addresses the second. The document also says the nature and speed of reporting to and decision-making by boards and senior management need to evolve.

Put that next to the execution-speed discussion and a practical question emerges for any leadership team. Who has tested whether the change-window and third-party coordination steps work at the pace the threat now demands? The document comes close to asking it: it says assurance activities are most valuable when they assess not just technical capabilities, such as how fast a firm can patch, but also the effectiveness and speed of leadership decisions.

## Financial stability and shared resources

The widest-angle point in the publication concerns the system as a whole. AI-related cyber risks could threaten financial stability if they affect shared resources used by many firms. The document names those resources: critical third parties, and common data, infrastructure and software, including open source. It's hedged with "could", and it should stay that way. The source describes a possibility the regulators take seriously, not an event that has occurred.

The logic follows from the earlier points. If open-source and open-weight dependencies may be widely embedded in other software and not immediately visible, then a weakness in one of them can be a weakness in many firms at once. Faster discovery and exploitation by threat actors raises the stakes of that concentration.

The document's response is collective. It says cooperation across borders and sectors, with collective defences operating at machine speed, is essential to manage these risks, and it points to fora such as the Cross-Market Operational Resilience Group (CMORG) and the Frontier AI Information Sharing Forum (FAISF). A single firm's exposure management, however good, can't fully address a weakness in a shared component. The publication places part of the answer outside any one organisation's perimeter.

## How I'd read this as a technical leader

Three things stand out, and I'll argue for them rather than merely list them.

First, the document is right to stop treating discovery as the hard part and to redirect attention to execution. Its own words, that discovery has historically been the main bottleneck, suggest to me that if the bottleneck moves, so should the budget. Teams that measure success by the number of findings are measuring the wrong end of the pipeline, and the document's advice on measuring harness effectiveness makes the same point.

Second, the status of the document deserves respect without over-reading. The practices aren't formal expectations, and the regulators say so. But the SS/FG expectations apply regardless of the technology, and the practices provide more detail on areas relevant to AI. A firm that ignores the practices isn't in breach of them. It may, though, find it harder to show that it has thought about the changed landscape.

Third, the human element isn't a footnote. The document says humans remain central to advanced cyber threat activity, and it says appropriate human oversight remains essential, especially for key decisions. Both sides of the contest still involve people. What changes is how quickly those people can act, and how reliably they can check the outputs of systems that may not give the same answer twice.

## What the document leaves open

A careful reader will notice what the claims here don't settle. The document says firms' traditional defences may struggle, but it doesn't say which will. It says defender and attacker capabilities aren't always symmetrical, but not how far they diverge. It notes that AISI sees safeguards improving while vulnerabilities remain, without saying how those trends net out for any given firm.

The question I'd carry forward is practical. If execution speed is the new constraint, and it spans identification, testing, change windows, third-party coordination, deployment and verification, then the weakest step in that chain sets the pace for everything else. For most firms, is that step technical at all? The document's own levers for the third-party step are contractual and collective: service levels for vulnerability remediation, prompt notification of changes to AI systems, and attention to whether smaller but material third parties can cope with a high volume of discovered vulnerabilities. None of those is something an AI system can speed up on its own. The publication frames the problem clearly enough that firms can begin measuring the answer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Who published the effective practices, and when?**
The Prudential Regulation Authority, the Financial Conduct Authority and the Bank of England issued the publication jointly on 19 November 2026. It covers firms and financial market infrastructures, using "firm" in the same way as the regulators' SS/FG on cyber risk and resilience management.

**Are the effective practices binding on firms?**
No. The publication says they are complementary to, but separate from, the SS/FG on cyber risk and resilience management, and are not part of the regulators' formal expectations. The SS/FG expectations apply regardless of the technology firms use, and firms can adopt the practices in a manner appropriate to their size, complexity and risk profile.

**What is the difference between vulnerability management and exposure management?**
The publication describes vulnerability management as periodically identifying and addressing vulnerabilities. Exposure management involves identifying exposures, defined as any misconfigurations, security gaps or weaknesses that a threat actor could exploit, and managing them systematically, with a shift from periodic identification to continuous exposure monitoring.

**What does the publication mean by execution speed?**
It is the speed at which firms identify affected assets, test remediation, secure a change window, coordinate with third parties, deploy remediation and verify its success. The publication says effective practice may require firms to increase it, in addition to faster decision-making by boards and senior management.

**Why do the regulators link AI cyber risk to financial stability?**
The publication says AI-related cyber risks could threaten financial stability if they affect shared resources used by many firms, including critical third parties and common data, infrastructure and software, including open source. It says cooperation across borders and sectors, with collective defences operating at machine speed, is essential to manage these risks.
