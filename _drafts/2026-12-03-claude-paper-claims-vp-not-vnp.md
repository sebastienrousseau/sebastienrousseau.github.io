# VP ≠ VNP: What the Claude Paper Claims and What Is Still Unchecked

**A paper credited to an internal research Claude model claims to prove VP ≠ VNP over every field, the algebraic analogue of P ≠ NP. Its proofs are formalised in Lean, but no human mathematician has checked them, and it does not settle P ≠ NP.**

## A claimed answer to a problem open since Valiant

Arithmetic circuits are a model of computation in which the gates are addition and multiplication operations rather than AND, OR and NOT. An arithmetic circuit works over a field, such as the real numbers, and it must work for every element of that field and of its extensions, not only for the inputs 0 and 1.

Valiant conjectured, in work from 1979 and 1982, that the permanent and the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial cannot be computed by polynomial-size arithmetic circuits. The paper describes the VP versus VNP problem as equivalent to that question. It reports a lower bound of 2^Ω((log m)^(4/3)), which it says is superpolynomial and thus separates VP from VNP.

The headline statement is "VP ≠ VNP over every field". The breadth matters: a separation over one convenient field would be a result about that field, whereas the paper claims the separation does not depend on which field the coefficients come from.

The paper is just as explicit about what it does not do. It calls the result the algebraic analogue of the conjecture P ≠ NP, "but it does not settle that conjecture". Proving VP = VNP over an appropriate field would imply that P/poly = NP/poly, the version of P = NP for Boolean circuits, a result due to Bürgisser, but no implication in the other direction is known. The paper adds that its superpolynomial bound does not apply to Boolean circuits, and that its results "say nothing about whether NP-complete problems have fast algorithms". Anyone reading this as news about P ≠ NP has misread it.

## Who wrote it, and who has checked it

The abstract states that the result "was discovered by an internal research Claude model and has been formalized in Lean". Section 3 goes further: "The proofs in this paper were found and written by AI. No human mathematician has checked the proofs."

The Lean side is described in detail. Every numbered statement in the paper has a Lean counterpart proved from Mathlib, with one exception covered below. The development uses Lean 4.33.1, contains no `sorry` outside the file that states the theorems for comparison, and depends on no axioms other than propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound. The paper reports that the Comparator tool accepted the proofs, rechecking them with Lean's kernel and with an independently written checker, nanoda.

That is strong evidence that the Lean proofs prove the Lean statements. It is not the same thing as evidence that the Lean statements say what the paper claims. The paper prints the statement file so that readers can check it, and its appendix says plainly: "Whether the statements say what is meant was judged only by AI readers. No human mathematician has checked the development, the statement file or the proofs." The repository README makes the same point: "Lean checks the proofs, but no machine checks that this file says what is claimed."

My reading is that the formalisation changes where scrutiny should go rather than removing the need for it. A human expert no longer has to trust 70 pages of argument line by line, but someone still has to confirm that the definitions of circuits, of the two polynomials and of Valiant's classes in the statement file match the standard ones. Until that happens, "the paper claims" is the accurate verb.

## The permanent, the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial and characteristic two

The paper names the permanent and the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial as the canonical VNP-complete problems. For an m × m matrix of variables, the permanent sums over all m! permutations; the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial HC_m sums over the (m − 1)! cyclic permutations. The permanent is VNP-complete for fields of characteristic other than 2, while the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial is VNP-complete for all fields.

That difference shapes the main theorem. The claimed bound holds for the permanent over every field of characteristic other than two, and for the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial over every field. The second polynomial is what lets the paper state the separation over every field without exception.

Characteristic two is handled carefully. There, the permanent coincides with the determinant, and since the determinant has polynomial-size circuits (Berkowitz showed it can be computed with a polynomial number of additions and multiplications), the theorem does not apply. The paper explains that its reduction to the permanent uses matrix entries of ±1/2 and so needs 2 ≠ 0, whereas the reduction to HC_m needs no division. This is not a gap in the argument; a bound for the permanent in characteristic two would be false, so the paper does not claim one.

One convention to keep in mind: the paper states that log means log₂ throughout.

## How large the claimed bound is

The theorem says there is an absolute constant c > 0 such that, over every field and for all large enough m, every circuit for HC_m, and every circuit for the permanent when the characteristic is not 2, has more than m^(c(log m)^(1/3)) gates.

The paper calls this quasipolynomial and contrasts it with the best known upper bounds, which are exponential, of the form 2^O(m), citing Ryser, Glynn and a 2026 preprint by Curticapean. In its own words, "there is still plenty of room to tighten the bounds." It also says: "We do not claim that the exponent 1/3 is optimal."

So the claim is that the permanent needs superpolynomial size, not a statement of its exact circuit complexity. Anyone hoping for a tight answer will not find one here.

## What the field knew before, according to the paper

The paper's survey of earlier work explains why a superpolynomial bound for general circuits would be a large step. To the authors' knowledge, the best previous lower bound for an explicit polynomial of degree d in n variables is Ω(n log d), due to Strassen and to Baur and Strassen. The permanent has m² variables and degree m, which gives m² log m, and the paper says no lower bound of larger order was known for it. That hedge, "to our knowledge", is the paper's, and it is worth keeping.

The paper places a recent result by Raz alongside this: for circuits over noncommutative rings, an explicit polynomial of degree n in n variables needs Ω(n^1.5) product gates. That is a different setting from circuits over a field, which is what the main theorem concerns.

For circuits over a field, the paper says bounds stronger than Ω(n log d) were known only for restricted models:

- formulas and algebraic branching programs, with bounds that are polynomial, at most quadratic in the number of variables;
- monotone circuits (Jerrum and Snir, Yehudayoff) and noncommutative formulas and branching programs (Nisan), with exponential lower bounds;
- homogeneous depth-four circuits, with bounds exponential in the square root of the degree, first with bounded bottom fan-in (Gupta, Kamath, Kayal and Saptharishi) and then in general (Kayal, Limaye, Saha and Srinivasan; Kumar and Saraf);
- multilinear formulas, with superpolynomial lower bounds (Raz);
- circuits of every constant depth, with superpolynomial lower bounds in characteristic zero or large characteristic, which the paper calls "a breakthrough of Limaye, Srinivasan and Tavenas", extended to all fields by Forbes.

Against that list, the paper says Theorem 1.1 "concerns general circuits, with no restriction on their depth, on their constants or on cancellations", and contrasts its superpolynomial bound with the Baur and Strassen bound, which is linear in the number of variables up to a logarithmic factor. My own gloss: each restricted model above gives up something a general circuit can do, and the claimed theorem gives up none of it. Cancellation, where a circuit builds large intermediate terms that later subtract away, is the usual reason general circuits are hard to analyse.

## Why the geometric complexity theory route was not taken

Geometric complexity theory (GCT), the programme of Mulmuley and Sohoni, approaches the border version of the conjecture through representation theory and orbit closures. The paper notes that occurrence obstructions, on which the original plan relied, cannot give superpolynomial bounds, as shown by Ikenmeyer and Panova and by Bürgisser, Ikenmeyer and Panova.

The paper says its proof does not follow this programme, "since it uses neither representation theory nor orbit closures". It does bear on the programme's target, though. The claimed bound for approximating circuits (Theorem 1.4) would imply that the permanent is not approximated by determinants of polynomial size, which over the complex numbers Mulmuley and Sohoni conjectured in the language of orbit closures.

The paper defines approximation precisely. A circuit over the field of formal Laurent series k((ε)) approximates a polynomial f over k if it computes f + εg, where every coefficient of g lies in k[[ε]]. Over the reals this captures limits: f is a limit of polynomials with small circuits, even if f has no small circuit itself. Since an exact circuit is also an approximating one, a lower bound against approximating circuits is the stronger statement.

That consequence for determinantal complexity is the one numbered statement of the paper that is not formalised in Lean (Remark N.6). Every other numbered statement has a Lean equivalent.

## A busy year for permanent lower bounds

The paper places its result among other 2026 work on the permanent versus the determinant. For the determinantal complexity of the m × m permanent, the classical lower bound is quadratic in m (Mignon and Ressayre; Cai, Chen and Li; Yabe), also for the border version (Landsberg, Manivel and Ressayre).

In 2026, according to the paper, a cubic lower bound, also for the border version over the complex numbers, was announced in a manuscript of OpenAI that comes with a Lean development. Karthik Sheshadri extended it to odd characteristic, and Jizhou Guo derived from the same manuscript a lower bound for permanent circuits with division. Sheshadri also announced a bound quadratic in the number of variables for the power sum, in a paper whose title describes it as fully Lean-verified, and Mrinal Kumar and Ben Lee Volk gave a short proof of a quadratic lower bound on determinantal complexity.

Several of these 2026 results arrive with Lean developments. I won't call that a trend on the strength of one paper's bibliography. For anyone building on mathematical or algorithmic foundations, though, a claim that arrives with a checkable formal proof gives a reviewer something concrete to check, provided someone checks the statements as well as the proofs.

## Spectral points instead of ranks

The technical approach, as the paper outlines it, starts from a known barrier: rank methods cannot give strong lower bounds for the rank of tensors (Efremenko, Garg, Oliveira and Wigderson; Garg, Makam, Oliveira and Wigderson). Many of the earlier restricted-model proofs bound the rank of a matrix of partial derivatives or of coefficients, following Nisan and Nisan and Wigderson.

The proof splits into two cases, which the paper calls the flat case and the contracting case, and shows that over every field at least one of them holds. It adds: "We do not know which of the two hypotheses holds, even over R." In the flat case, the paper uses, instead of ranks, functionals in a variant of Strassen's asymptotic spectrum, which it calls spectral points.

Few points of the asymptotic spectrum are known explicitly: the ranks of flattenings, the support functionals on oblique tensors and, over the complex numbers, the quantum functionals. The spectral points the paper uses "are not explicit at all, since they are only known to exist by Zorn's lemma".

This is a non-constructive argument, and the Lean development is consistent with that: Classical.choice is among the three axioms it uses. In practical terms, the proof, if correct, shows that a certain obstruction exists without exhibiting a concrete one. Anyone hoping to extract an explicit hardness certificate from it will not find one there.

## Barriers, and what remains open

The paper comments briefly on known barriers. Algebraically natural proofs, the analogue of Razborov and Rudich's natural proofs, may be unable to prove superpolynomial lower bounds for general circuits, and the paper says there is evidence in both directions, citing work by Chatterjee, Kumar, Ramya, Saptharishi and Tengse and by Kumar, Ramya, Saptharishi and Tengse. It also says the authors "have not analysed" how the argument relates to relativisation and algebrisation, and have "not worked out" what the bound gives for derandomising polynomial identity testing.

Several items stay open on the paper's own account. The bound is quasipolynomial against exponential upper bounds. The exponent 1/3 is not claimed to be optimal. Characteristic two is covered only through the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial. Which of the two cases holds over a given field is unknown. And P ≠ NP is untouched: it is not known whether Valiant's conjecture implies Boolean separations such as P ≠ NP.

The largest open item is the one the paper states about itself: no human mathematician has checked the proofs or the Lean statement file. The question I would most like answered, once that review happens, is the one the authors' caveat raises: if the exponent 1/3 is not optimal, how far can spectral points be pushed, given how little of the asymptotic spectrum anyone can write down explicitly?

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Has VP ≠ VNP now been proved?**
A paper credited to an internal research Claude model claims to prove it, and its proofs are formalised in Lean. The paper itself states that no human mathematician has checked the proofs, the Lean development or the statement file, so the result should be treated as a claim until mathematicians review it.

**Does this settle P ≠ NP?**
No. The paper calls VP ≠ VNP the algebraic analogue of P ≠ NP and says it does not settle that conjecture. Its bound does not apply to Boolean circuits, and it is not known whether Valiant's conjecture implies Boolean separations such as P ≠ NP.

**What does the Lean formalisation guarantee?**
Lean, with Mathlib, checks that the proofs prove the formal statements, using only the axioms propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound, and the paper reports a second check with the independent nanoda kernel. It does not check that the formal statements match the paper's mathematical claims; the paper says that was judged only by AI readers.

**Why does the permanent result exclude characteristic two?**
In characteristic two the permanent coincides with the determinant, which has polynomial-size circuits, so no superpolynomial bound can hold there. The paper covers that case with the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial, which it says is VNP-complete over every field.

**How strong is the claimed bound?**
It is quasipolynomial: more than m^(c(log m)^(1/3)) gates for an m × m matrix and large m. The best known upper bounds are exponential, and the paper does not claim the exponent 1/3 is optimal.
