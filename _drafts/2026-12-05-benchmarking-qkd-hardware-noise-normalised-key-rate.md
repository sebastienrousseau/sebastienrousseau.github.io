# A Fairer Way to Compare Quantum Key Distribution Hardware

**A published secret key rate mixes the quality of the hardware with the conditions of the channel; a new preprint proposes the noise-normalised key rate, a per-pulse figure that divides the channel out and stays flat across a wide range of loss.**

## Why a headline key rate tells you so little

Anyone who has tried to line up QKD results from the literature will recognise the problem. One paper quotes a key rate at one distance, another at a different wavelength, a third with classical data running alongside in the same fibre. The numbers are all real, and they are hard to set against each other.

The paper puts it plainly: every experiment reports its secret key rate (SKR) at its own distance, wavelength, launch power and clock rate, so each reported value combines the quality of the hardware with the conditions of the channel. The SKR remains the figure that matters for a given link; the difficulty is that each published value is measured under conditions its authors chose. A high number might reflect excellent equipment, or a short, quiet fibre. The headline figure cannot tell you which.

That matters more as QKD moves into national infrastructure. The authors note that all 27 European Union member states have signed the declaration of the European Quantum Communication Infrastructure (EuroQCI), that many of these networks are planned on installed fibre carrying classical traffic, and that co-propagation of quantum and classical signals has been demonstrated across protocols, fibre types and wavelength bands, with classical capacities up to 18 Tb/s. The question they pose is a practical one: on the same channel, which implementation extracts more secret key?

The paper is a preprint by Jerry Horgan, Indrakshi Dey, Hajar Bennouri and Daniel Kilper, and it has not yet completed peer review. The figures below are the authors' own.

## Dividing out the operating conditions

The factorisation is simple to state. The SKR is the product of the transmitter clock rate, the channel transmittance and a third term, the noise-normalised key rate (NNKR). The first two are operating conditions and are divided out. What remains collects the hardware factors: in the authors' words, it is how much secret key the hardware extracts from each pulse that reaches the receiver. Equivalently, NNKR is the secret key per transmitted pulse divided by the probability that a pulse survives the channel.

Transmittance has a plain definition. The fibre transmits a fraction η = 10^(−αL/10) of the light, where α is the attenuation in dB/km and L the length. The four inputs NNKR needs are therefore the SKR, the clock rate, α and L, all of which papers usually state. One consequence is worth spelling out: multiplexer, filter and connector losses belong to the setup, so NNKR counts them as hardware.

For decoy-state BB84, NNKR expands into a product of physical factors: the mean photon number per pulse (µ), the receiver optics transmission, the detector efficiency, the sifting efficiency and the secure fraction. Sifting means keeping the detections in which sender and receiver used the same measurement basis; the secure fraction κ is the number of secret bits per sifted bit after error correction and privacy amplification, which shortens the key to remove any information an eavesdropper may hold. κ falls as the quantum bit error rate (QBER) rises. In decibels the product becomes a sum, which is what lets NNKR read as a penalty budget.

## Reference points: what ideal hardware scores

A metric in decibels needs anchors, and the paper supplies two. Ideal single-photon BB84 with standard sifting (every factor equal to 1 except a sifting efficiency of one half) sits at −3.0 dB. An ideal decoy-state system with weak coherent pulses and biased basis choice reaches about 0.30, or −5.2 dB, at a typical mean photon number of 0.5, with sifting efficiency close to 1.

Those anchors matter because they show how far real systems sit from ideal, and why. The highest record in the comparison, a superconducting-detector system by Li et al., reaches −11.3 dB at its 10 km point, which the authors read as 6–7 dB of remaining headroom below the decoy-state ceiling, spread across detector efficiency, timing jitter, error-correction efficiency and finite-size effects.

## Relating NNKR to the repeaterless bound

QKD researchers already have a benchmark for how well a system uses its channel. The bound of Pirandola, Laurenza, Ottaviani and Banchi (PLOB) gives the maximum secret key per channel use of a lossy repeaterless channel, and the PLOB fraction divides a system's secret key per pulse by that capacity.

The paper ties its metric to this fraction. At high loss, NNKR equals the PLOB fraction divided by ln 2, so the two agree up to a constant. They part ways at low loss, because the capacity grows faster than the transmittance. For fixed hardware, the authors calculate that the PLOB fraction is 2.0 dB lower at 2 dB of loss than its high-loss value, 0.8 dB lower at 5 dB and 0.2 dB lower at 10 dB. Over 2–25 dB that is the 1.6-fold variation in the abstract. NNKR, by construction, does not move.

The practical effect is easy to see. Compare a metro link measured at 2 dB with a long link measured at 25 dB, and the PLOB fraction builds in a 2 dB artefact of the test conditions; NNKR does not. The authors also prove that dividing by the transmittance is, up to a constant factor, the only normalisation of this form that stays constant in the loss-limited regime for every system. A hardware figure of merit that moves when you change the fibre is partly measuring the fibre, and this is, to my mind, the strongest argument for the metric.

## Where flat stops being flat

NNKR is not flat at every loss, and the paper is explicit about where it breaks. At low loss, detector saturation, dead time or key-processing throughput can set the SKR, so NNKR rises as the transmittance falls. Near the reach limit, dark counts, afterpulses and, in shared fibre, Raman photons approach the signal detection rate, the error rate rises and NNKR rolls off. NNKR is also not constant for protocols with a different rate–loss scaling, such as twin-field QKD, although the authors note the framework carries over with an adjusted exponent.

An analytic key-rate model gives the hardware constant in closed form and the edges of the loss range over which NNKR is flat. The separation between those edges is set by the product of the detector noise count rate and dead time, divided by the number of detectors, and by nothing else: the flat range is a property of the detectors. To turn this into a measurement procedure, the authors define a 1 dB plateau, by analogy with the 1 dB bandwidth of an optical component, as the loss range around the NNKR maximum over which NNKR stays within 1 dB of it.

The paper reports a practical consequence for published records. The six distances reported by Fan-Yuan et al. give a 1 dB plateau from 4.7 to 15.8 dB of loss, and their 10 km record point lies 1.0 dB below it, where those authors identify detector afterpulsing as the limit. Grünenfelder et al. rise by 1.5 dB in NNKR from their 10 km point to their 102 km point, and the paper attributes 1.0 dB of that to lower detector efficiency at the higher count rate of the short link. The authors' conclusion is that the 10 km benchmark at which SKR records are quoted places high-rate systems in their detector-saturation regime, and that NNKR read on the plateau gives their hardware value, 1.0–1.5 dB above the record point.

## A companion metric for noisy channels

NNKR answers what the hardware extracts per received pulse. It does not account for how much noise the channel itself adds, which matters when classical traffic shares the fibre. For that the paper introduces the implementation efficiency (IE), which divides the SKR by the key that ideal equipment would extract from the same lossy, noisy channel, computed from the closed-form coexistence limits of Zischler et al.

The dominant noise mechanism is spontaneous Raman scattering, in which molecular vibrations of the glass shift a small fraction of the classical light into the quantum channel. For BB84, the ratio of IE to NNKR depends only on the modelled noise error rate: it equals 2 on a noise-free channel and grows with the noise. Across the five low-noise BB84 experiments in the study it is 2.00–2.03, and 2.23 for the experiment with the highest classical power. Two caveats from the paper belong here. The reference counts every noise detection as an error, which gives an upper estimate of IE. And coexistence limits exist only for BB84 and Gaussian-modulated continuous-variable QKD, so IE is not given for the coherent one-way (COW) and measurement-device-independent entries.

The paper also positions its metrics against existing ones. The co-propagation efficiency (CE) of Gavignet et al. measures coexistence capacity and by design scales with fibre length and classical launch power; Bebrov's efficiency counts the resources a protocol consumes; ITU-T Recommendation Y.3807 lists quality-of-service parameters. Each answers a different question. The authors write that, to their knowledge, a per-pulse hardware metric of this kind has not previously been tested for loss invariance on a multi-vendor testbed, or used to place coexistence experiments, production systems and laboratory records on one scale. That is a statement about what they found, not a proof that nothing similar exists.

## The evidence base

The analysis draws on four data sets. The literature set is ten published coexistence demonstrations, chosen to span four protocols, four fibre types, the O and C bands and classical capacities from 10 Gb/s to 18 Tb/s. The state-of-the-art set is four high-rate laboratory systems. The testbed set is 42,115 SKR values logged by four systems on the TCD/CONNECT testbed during automated attenuation sweeps: a Toshiba production unit (System A), a Toshiba research unit (System B), an ID Quantique Clavis3 running COW (System C) and an ID Quantique ClavisXGR running decoy-state BB84 (System D). The coexistence set is a Toshiba system measured over 0 and 50 km of fibre, at baseline and with 12 dBm of classical injection.

The breadth is the point. A metric that worked on only one kind of system would be far less useful. The paper backs the computation with consistency checks, a chi-squared test of cross-laboratory agreement and an explicit uncertainty analysis.

## What the production data show

The headline production result is about stability under changing loss. System A holds NNKR at −19.0 dB over a 17.0 dB plateau, from 8.9 to 25.9 dB, with a coefficient of variation (standard deviation divided by mean) of 6.9%. Seventeen decibels is a factor of about fifty in transmitted light. Over each testbed plateau, NNKR removes 96–98% of the loss dependence that the SKR shows. The fitted model places System A's plateau at 8.6–25.7 dB, against the measured 8.9–25.9 dB, with a root-mean-square residual of 0.28 dB.

The second test is agreement with the outside world. System A's value agrees within 0.7 dB with three published deployments of the same platform (Patel 2014, Dynes 2016 and Pistoia 2023), and the four values pass the chi-squared test (p = 0.56). The fourth published deployment, Gavignet 2024, sits lower, at −23.7 dB, and the paper explains why: that system was configured and operated to carry 6 Tb/s at 16.8 dBm, the highest classical power of the ten experiments, and the cost appears as a lower efficiency. The authors also note that all four published deployments include Toshiba co-authors, so the agreement shows the reproducibility of one vendor's platform rather than independent replication.

Not every system behaves as neatly. System D delivers 4.7–5.9 kb/s from 6 to 20 dB, nearly independent of loss, which the authors read as a rate ceiling; its plateau at −31.5 dB is therefore a lower bound on its hardware constant, which the model puts at −26.3 dB.

## Does coexistence break the metric?

Coexistence is where deployments are heading, so the paper asks whether either metric degrades when a production system shares its fibre with classical traffic. Comparing each VOA setting on the baseline plateau with and without 12 dBm of injection, NNKR changes by −0.35 ± 0.21 dB at 0 km and −0.45 ± 0.24 dB at 50 km. The seven individual changes range from −0.98 to +0.27 dB, with a mean of −0.39 dB. IE at 50 km changes by between −0.45 and +0.03 dB, depending on the noise model.

So the averages stay within 0.5 dB, half the 1 dB plateau criterion, though one individual setting moved by nearly 1 dB at the edge of the plateau where roll-off begins. The classical light does affect the curves beyond the plateau: at 0 km and 27 dB of attenuation, NNKR with injection lies 16.4 dB below baseline. The paper's summary is that coexistence shortens the plateau but does not change its level. As with the production figures, this is a result for one platform, not a blanket statement about all systems.

## Treating the hardware as a link budget

Optical engineers think in link budgets: a launch power, a list of penalties in decibels, a received level at the end. NNKR fits that habit. The paper tests the analogy on the 13.7 Mb/s system of Yuan et al.: a mean photon number of 0.4 (−4.0 dB), a 2 dB receiver interferometer loss, a 31% detector efficiency (−5.1 dB), a sifting efficiency of 0.90 (−0.5 dB) and a measured secure fraction of 0.29 (−5.4 dB) sum to −16.9 dB, against a measured −16.6 dB. Separately, the analytic model predicts a secure fraction of 0.273 against the measured 0.292, also within 0.3 dB.

That makes the decomposition more than bookkeeping. The same budget accounts for the observed 2.0 dB advantage of Fan-Yuan et al. within 0.8 dB from their more efficient detectors and lower receiver loss. It also places production systems on the same scale as records: the production deployments and System A sit at −19.0 to −19.7 dB, 2.4–3.1 dB below the same vendor's 13.7 Mb/s research system. A single efficiency number tells you that one system is worse than another. A budget suggests which block to look at first, and I think that diagnostic quality is where the metric earns its keep for engineers evaluating equipment.

## What this changes in practice

For anyone writing a QKD paper, the implication is straightforward: report the four inputs and the NNKR alongside the SKR, and, for IE, the classical launch power, channel count, quantum–classical separation, receiver bandwidth and detection gate, propagation direction and operating QBER, which the paper lists as a minimum reporting set. The cost is small, and a reader can then place the work against others without reverse-engineering the operating conditions.

For anyone evaluating vendors, the paper offers three tools: a hardware term that does not move with loss inside its plateau, an efficiency measured against the ideal key of the same noisy channel, and a budget that points to the component responsible. None of them requires trusting a headline rate quoted at a flattering distance. The authors are clear that NNKR complements rather than replaces the SKR: reporting the clock rate alongside NNKR still gives the full picture, since the SKR is their product with the transmittance.

The limits deserve stating plainly. The paper is a preprint. The 6.9% coefficient of variation and the coexistence result are for one production platform. The 0.3 dB budget closure is for one laboratory system. The flat range has edges, so a measurement taken outside them will understate the hardware. And the cross-deployment agreement covers deployments that share a vendor and its co-authors.

## An open question the data raise

The most interesting loose end is also the most practical. If record-setting experiments are quoted at operating points where the detectors limit the hardware score, then the published records may not be the best statement of what the equipment can do. Because the model gives the edges of the flat range in terms of detector noise count rate and dead time, those edges can be estimated before an experiment is run. Whether future high-rate experiments will be designed to sit inside that range, and report NNKR to show it, is a question the next round of records will answer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the noise-normalised key rate (NNKR)?**
It is the secret key rate divided by the transmitter clock rate and the channel transmittance: the secret key the hardware extracts per pulse that reaches the receiver. It needs four routinely reported parameters (SKR, clock rate, fibre attenuation and length) and, in decibels, reads as a sum of source, receiver, detector and key-processing penalties.

**Why not use the fraction of the PLOB bound instead?**
At high loss the two agree up to a constant (NNKR equals the PLOB fraction divided by ln 2). At low loss the PLOB fraction of fixed hardware falls, by 2.0 dB at 2 dB of loss, so it varies 1.6-fold over 2–25 dB. NNKR does not change with loss for fixed loss-limited hardware.

**Does NNKR stay constant at every distance?**
No. At low loss, detector saturation, dead time or processing throughput can cap the key rate, and near the reach limit noise counts raise the error rate. The paper defines a 1 dB plateau and shows that its width is set by the detectors' noise count rate and dead time.

**What is the implementation efficiency (IE)?**
It divides the measured key rate by the key ideal equipment would extract from the same lossy, noisy channel, using closed-form coexistence limits. It is available for BB84 and Gaussian-modulated continuous-variable QKD, not for COW or MDI-QKD, and it gives an upper estimate because every noise detection is counted as an error.

**Is the paper peer reviewed?**
Not yet. It was posted to arXiv on 20 November 2026 and is under review at the IEEE Journal of Lightwave Technology.
