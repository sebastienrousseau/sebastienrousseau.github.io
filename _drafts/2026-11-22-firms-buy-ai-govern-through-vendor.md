# Firms Buy Their Most Material AI and Govern It Through the Vendor

**The Bank of England and FCA survey shows UK financial firms buying a growing share of their AI, including most of their high-materiality systems, and understanding those bought systems mainly through vendor assurance and contracts rather than the deep knowledge they hold of what they build.**

## A survey that describes rather than prescribes

The Bank of England and the Financial Conduct Authority conducted a fourth survey of artificial intelligence and machine learning in UK financial services, and the publication is explicit about its own limits. It provides a descriptive summary of the aggregate survey results, and its findings "should be read as evidence from participating firms, rather than as a complete assessment of AI adoption across the whole UK financial sector".

The base matters. The survey received 196 responses, from 101 larger firms and 95 smaller ones. Firms with fewer than 100 employees could complete a shorter questionnaire, and 105 respondents completed the full survey. Unless otherwise stated, the figures are based on the full survey. The authors also warn that comparisons with 2024 "should therefore be interpreted with care where question wording, definitions, scope or respondent populations differ". When a number moves between 2024 and 2026, I put the two figures side by side and keep that warning in view.

My own reading, which is framing rather than fact, is that the most consequential thread in the results is third-party dependence. The introduction says firms "are increasingly reporting use of generative AI, AI-enabled agents and third-party implementations of AI systems". Of those three, the third connects most directly to governance, to resilience and to what respondents say worries them. The sections below follow that thread.

## Where the systems sit, and who has most of them

Overall, 86% of respondents reported using AI, up from 75% in 2024, though the authors note the two percentages do not relate to the same population of firms. Generative AI accounted for 56% of all reported AI systems in 2026. In 2024, foundation models accounted for 17% of reported AI use cases. The survey says these measures "capture different aspects of AI adoption and are therefore not directly comparable", while taken together they suggest a substantial increase in reported use of general-purpose capabilities.

By business area, Operations and IT accounted for approximately 31% of all reported AI systems, up from around 22% in 2024. No other individual business area accounted for more than 11%, so the largest area is at least nearly three times the size of any other. By domain, enterprise productivity, such as coding assistants or summarisation tools, was the most widely reported current use, selected by 86% of respondents.

The distribution across firms is uneven. Almost half of firms reported ten or fewer AI systems, while the ten firms reporting the most systems accounted for 43% of all reported AI systems. Around 10% of firms reported over 100. A headline count of systems therefore reflects a small number of very large estates alongside many modest ones.

Materiality tells a subtler story. Around 7% of reported AI systems were assessed by respondents as high materiality, compared with 16% in 2024. Read in isolation, that looks like a fall in the importance of AI to firms. The survey rules out that reading: the absolute number of high-materiality AI systems reported in 2026 was greater than in 2024. The share fell because the total grew faster. High-materiality systems were also concentrated, with ten firms accounting for 62% of them.

## Agents are widespread, and they mostly advise

Agents attract the most overheated commentary, so the numbers are worth stating plainly. 80% of respondents reported having AI agents currently deployed, and a further 14% reported plans to deploy them over the next 12 months.

The level of autonomy is the part that matters. 80% of respondents reported agents deployed for decision support, where agents recommend actions and humans decide and execute. 46% reported agents with constrained execution, 22% with conditional autonomy, and only 2% with broad autonomy. The survey's own summary is that "deployment is widespread, but autonomy is limited".

Customer-facing use is where the boundary between advice and action is being tested. 16% of respondents reported current use of agents for automated customer communications, rising to 34% when planned deployment was included. Even including planned deployment over the next 12 months, no more than 9% expected agents to approve or decline customer outcomes, execute trades or place orders, initiate, execute or block payments, or change limits, thresholds and controls.

## The third-party shift

Third-party implementations accounted for 39% of reported AI systems in 2026, compared with 33% in 2024 and 17% in 2022. The share of high-materiality systems moved further: third-party implementations accounted for 53% of high-materiality AI systems, up from 35% in 2024. That takes third parties from a minority to a majority of the systems that firms themselves rate as high materiality, and it happened while the absolute number of those systems grew.

The supplier base is concentrated. The three largest cloud and compute providers accounted for 79% of all reported cloud/compute relationships, and 98% of respondents reported use of one of them. The three largest model API/hosting providers accounted for 54% of reported relationships. Of the 169 reported distinct cloud and compute relationships, 37% were deemed both high criticality and hard to substitute, and only 35% of that group had tested exit plans.

Respondents are aware of the tension. Concerns about information security and/or operational resilience were the most frequently reported major constraint on third-party AI adoption, selected by 40% of respondents, followed by limited transparency into third-party AI systems at 34%. Counting major and moderate constraints together, difficulty negotiating contractual protections such as liability allocation, audit rights and advance notice of changes was the second most cited challenge.

## Buying is not treated like any other purchase

The title's claim needs care here, because the survey does not show firms treating bought AI as if they had built it. Most respondents applied additional evaluation and integration processes to third-party AI systems, rather than relying on the same procurement processes as for other third-party services. AI-specific risk identification and management, and additional security and data-protection controls, were the most widely reported measures.

The thinner layer is resilience. Enhanced operational-resilience expectations, such as joint incident testing or exit planning, were reported by 27% of firms. On vendor updates, 48% of respondents reported always undertaking regression testing for high-materiality updates to vendor-provided models or their guardrails, with a further 25% usually doing so. 73% reported always or usually having defined rollback plans, and 25% reported always or usually allowing high-materiality vendor updates to propagate automatically into production. Only 48 firms answered that question, and the survey says the results should be treated with caution.

## Two ways of understanding a system

The sharpest governance result is a comparison between internal and external high-materiality systems. 77% of respondents reported relying on deep knowledge of internally developed systems, compared with 24% for third-party systems. For third-party systems, 81% instead relied on vendor assurance and contractual arrangements. Assurance of behaviour and outcomes, and constrained autonomy, were widely reported for both kinds of system.

My interpretation is that firms understand what they build by knowing it, and what they buy through the supplier and the contract. Both are reasonable instruments, and the survey does not judge either. They are not the same thing as the direct knowledge that more than three-quarters of respondents rely on for their in-house high-materiality systems.

Meanwhile, the share of high-materiality systems arriving through the third-party route has grown from 35% to 53%. Respondents point to the same place. Oversight of third-party AI systems was the most commonly reported area of governance uncertainty or implementation challenge. Applying model risk management principles to third-party or vendor-supplied models was selected by 54% of respondents, and overseeing systems provided or materially influenced by third parties by 49%.

Those were selections from a list of possible challenges, so they are not a measure of failure. They do show where firms themselves locate the difficulty. If model risk management principles were written with in-house models in mind, as I would argue they largely were, the 54% figure is what you would expect when most high-materiality systems come from outside. The transparency constraint, at 34%, points the same way from a different question.

## What governance looks like on paper

Most firms have built the structure. 94% of respondents reported firmwide AI policies, principles or standards. Third-party risk-management frameworks, data-governance arrangements and senior oversight were each reported by at least three-quarters, and 84% reported centralised AI/model-risk governance fora. Fewer than half reported incident-management and response playbooks.

The data risk controls split into two tiers. Pre-deployment review, human approval and vendor due diligence were each reported by more than 90% of respondents. Ongoing monitoring, leakage testing and periodic review or recertification were each reported by around half. Around two-fifths of respondents also identified independent validation, ongoing monitoring and drift detection, or change control and versioning as governance challenges.

The shape is front-loaded: gating a system on the way in is near-universal, and the checks that show whether it still behaves as expected later are reported by about half. The survey does not say why, and I won't guess. For bought systems that change through vendor updates, that later stage is where the vendor's assurance meets the firm's own evidence.

## What firms expect next

Operational efficiency, automation and productivity were the highest-rated areas of perceived benefit from AI today, though none of the listed benefits was widely considered to be more than moderate in scale. Respondents expected benefits to increase across all areas over the next three years, with the largest anticipated increases in cybersecurity and operational resilience, and in revenue generation and competitive positioning.

The highest-rated areas of perceived risk to firms from AI today were data protection and security, third-party and supply chain risk, and GenAI-specific risks. Respondents expected risk levels to increase across all categories over the next one to three years, although on average they rated risks as less severe in 2026 than in 2024.

The systemic view is where third parties stand out. Cyber risk amplification generated the greatest current concern, with 57% of respondents rating it high or severe, rising to 80% for the one to three years ahead. Critical third-party dependencies followed, rising from 40% to 63%. These were the two highest-rated systemic risks both today and over the next three years.

The authors frame the balance in hedged terms. AI developments "may bring benefits" including greater efficiency, improved decision support and stronger risk management, and "may also create or amplify risks" including third-party dependency and financial stability. The word "may" appears in both sentences, and it should stay there in any retelling.

## Frontier cyber capability and the supply chain

88% of respondents assessed evolving frontier AI cyber capabilities over the next two years as having a moderate or high impact on their firm's risk profile. That covers moderate and high together, so it does not tell us how many expect a severe effect.

When asked through which channel frontier AI currently posed the greatest risk, 44% selected faster exploitation of existing software vulnerabilities, followed by 19% selecting increased effectiveness of supply-chain or third-party attacks. 64% selected vulnerability management and patching cadence as the area of cyber resilience that had received the greatest increase in management attention. Looking ahead, 45% expected to use AI-enabled defensive tooling in managing third-party risk exposures over the next two years.

## What is slowing firms down

The most cited non-regulatory constraints were difficulties integrating AI into existing business processes and decisions, and those imposed by legacy systems or infrastructure, identified as large constraints by 23% and 22% of respondents respectively. The most cited perceived regulatory constraints related to UK data protection and privacy, and international regulation, which 42% and 29% of respondents respectively perceived as posing a high compliance burden. The survey reports perceptions of burden, so it does not establish how heavy the rules actually are.

Small firms answered a shorter questionnaire. Among short-form respondents, 73% reported using AI, and of those, 58% reported predominantly using general-purpose large language models. Operations was the most commonly reported domain for current or planned use, at 64%, and 30% identified data protection, confidentiality and IT considerations as constraints.

## What a fifth survey could settle

The survey leaves a clear question for the next one. Third-party implementations have gone from 35% to 53% of high-materiality AI systems. Three cloud and compute providers cover 79% of reported cloud/compute relationships. Respondents rate critical third-party dependencies among the top two systemic risks for the next three years, and the most common governance challenge is oversight of exactly these systems.

What the descriptive data cannot tell us is whether vendor assurance and contractual arrangements, relied on by 81% of respondents for third-party high-materiality systems, are enough to carry that weight. Evidence on how bought systems behave in production would help answer it, and ongoing monitoring, reported by around half of respondents as a data risk control, is one control that could supply it. If the next survey shows monitoring and tested exit plans rising alongside the third-party share, firms will have started to close the gap this one records. If the share keeps climbing while they stay where they are, the gap will have widened.
