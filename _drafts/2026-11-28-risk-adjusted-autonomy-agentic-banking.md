# Supervising the Chain: Risk-Adjusted Autonomy for Agentic Banking

**A bank that checks whether its AI model gives accurate answers has answered the wrong question once that model can act. Supervision has to follow the whole decision-and-action chain, from the data that goes in to the transaction that comes out.**

## Why recommending and executing are different supervisory problems

The paper is *Governing Autonomous Finance: Data Trust, AI Agent Accountability and Prudential Resilience*, an independent research and policy paper by Bamidele Aly dated 22 October 2026.

Most debate about AI in finance still treats the model as the thing to be supervised. Is it accurate? Is it biased? Can it be explained? Those questions matter, but the paper argues they stop short once a system moves from advice to action.

Its reasoning is direct. Execution of a transaction, as distinct from recommending one, can create legal obligations, change market exposures, transfer assets, trigger liquidity demands and compress the time available to detect errors. The paper frames this with hedged language, and it's right to: these are things execution *can* do, not things it always does. But the list is enough to show why a recommendation engine and an acting agent belong in different supervisory categories.

From that follows a point I think is the paper's most useful reframing. The relevant unit of supervision isn't always the model alone; it can be the end-to-end decision-and-action chain. A model is one link. Supervisors who inspect only that link may miss what happens before it (the data it was given) and after it (the permissions that let its output become an instruction).

The paper puts the consequence sharply. Validation limited to output accuracy can't establish the safety of a system whose outputs can become instructions. And it offers a concrete example of how accuracy and safety come apart: a correct recommendation can be executed using the wrong account, an outdated agreement or an excessive permission. The model did nothing wrong, and the outcome is still bad.

## What counts as an agent

Precision about terms matters here, because "agent" is applied loosely. For the purposes of the paper, an AI agent is an AI-enabled system that can pursue an assigned objective through a sequence of decisions and tool-mediated actions within a defined environment.

The paper's illustration is deliberately plain. A chatbot that explains a CSA term isn't equivalent to an agent allowed to send a margin call, amend standing data or initiate an asset movement. The first informs a human. The second can change the bank's position.

Autonomy, the paper adds, is multidimensional. It covers discretion over objectives, selection of tools, access to information, initiation of actions and the ability to cross organisational boundaries. Two systems that both get called "autonomous" might differ along every one of those axes.

This leads to a practical consequence for anyone assessing risk. Describing an application as "agentic" is insufficient for risk assessment. Supervisors should instead ask which permissions the system possesses, where deterministic guardrails operate and what a human must authorise. Those are questions with answers a firm can document and a supervisor can test. The label alone tells them nothing.

The paper is careful not to overcorrect. Governance, it says, should scale with the consequences and controllability of the specific application, and neither BIS perspective it draws on implies that all agentic AI applications are high risk. That restraint is worth noting. A framework that rated every agent as dangerous would be easy to write and impossible to use.

## The comparison behind the framework

The paper's method is a structured qualitative comparison of international BIS analysis and selected instruments from the United Kingdom, Singapore and the European Union. It's stated plainly to be a qualitative documentary comparison, not a causal impact evaluation or legal opinion.

The selected sources are coded against common questions: scope and legal force, data quality, accountability, action permissions, runtime monitoring, and outsourcing, operational and financial stability issues. Using the same questions across every source is what keeps the comparison honest.

Two choices about scope deserve attention. First, the BIS is analysed as an international source of supervisory insight, not a fourth jurisdiction. It sits alongside the national and regional texts rather than competing with them. Second, the paper warns that treating the compared UK, Singapore and EU texts as interchangeable would obscure their different legal statuses and mandates. A guidance document and a binding rule aren't the same thing, and a comparison that flattened them would mislead.

The paper is also candid about one UK source. The FCA's retail-focused Mills Review doesn't replace the Bank of England/PRA's prudential responsibilities, so its application to wholesale collateral management is explicitly an analytical extension. The author doesn't pretend the review speaks directly to the case they study.

Two BIS sources anchor the international side. Crisanto et al. (2026) examine how data quality, privacy, security and third-party concentration shape AI-related risks in financial services. That paper, FSI Insights No. 73, identifies supervisory and policy approaches to AI data use, highlighting privacy, quality, security and third-party dependencies. Restoy (2026) calls for a wider supervisory lens encompassing operational and strategic resilience, including the effects of AI adoption beyond financial institutions on banks' customers and business models.

Taken together, these sources point towards data on one side and resilience on the other. The paper uses that combination as a rationale for an integrated chain-of-controls analysis. It's explicit that this is not a claim that the BIS has prescribed a detailed agentic transaction standard. That distinction matters: the paper builds on the sources without putting words in their mouths.

## What the comparison found

The finding is two-sided. There's considerable conceptual convergence on accountability and risk-based oversight. The sources broadly agree that someone must be answerable and that oversight should be proportionate to risk. But they differ in emphasis on three things: the moment of action, cross-framework coordination and the aggregation of correlated exposures.

Those three differences are where the paper locates its contribution, and it's precise about what that contribution is not. It isn't a claim that current rules leave all autonomous acts unregulated. It's the identification of an integration problem: effective prudential oversight requires evidence that controls over data and authority remain effective when agents transact at speed and scale.

I'd argue this is the more defensible position. "The rules don't cover agents" is a claim that a single counterexample can knock down. "The pieces exist but nobody has shown they hold together under speed and scale" is harder to dismiss, because it concerns the seams between instruments rather than the absence of any one.

## Data trust is necessary and not sufficient

The paper spends real effort on data, and its definition is demanding. In an autonomous workflow, "trusted data" must mean inputs that are current, identifiable, permissioned, reconciled and suitable for the specific proposed action. Each word does work. Data can be current but unidentifiable, or reconciled but unsuitable for the action in front of the agent.

One consequence is that a language model's own sense of certainty can't stand in for verification. Confidence scores generated by a language model are not a substitute for checking an authoritative source. In a collateral workflow, the paper says, the current signed CSA, agreed amendments, exposure calculation and dispute status should take precedence over a model's fluent interpretation. The word "fluent" is well chosen. A model can write a convincing reading of an agreement that differs from the signed one.

Yet the paper refuses to let data quality carry the whole argument. Data trust is not sufficient. A well-governed dataset can support an unauthorised action. A model with reliable inputs can have an overbroad permission scope. And a transaction can be individually valid yet collectively destabilising when many firms take similar actions.

Each of those failures slips past a data-quality check, which is why the proposed framework treats data quality as one pillar in a broader chain of accountability, rather than the final policy objective. For a policy discussion that often treats data as the headline concern, this is a pointed correction.

## Where the control boundary sits

If the unit of supervision is the chain, the control boundary has to be drawn across it. The paper states that the effective control boundary lies across data ingestion, reasoning, tool use, system integration and independent verification.

The encouraging part is what that implies for imperfect models. Strong transaction controls can substantially reduce the consequences of an imperfect model. That's a practical claim for founders and engineers: you don't have to wait for a flawless model if the controls around it limit what a mistake can do. A permission boundary that an agent can't exceed, or a deterministic check before an asset moves, bounds the damage whatever the model produces.

It's also why the earlier supervisory questions are the right ones. Which permissions does the system hold? Where do deterministic guardrails operate? What must a human authorise? Each maps to a place on the chain where a failure can be caught or allowed through.

## The five dimensions of the RAAF

The paper proposes the Risk-Adjusted Autonomy Framework with five analytical dimensions: information integrity, permitted authority, runtime controls, financial consequence and system-level propagation. They're best read as five questions applied to a given workflow.

Information integrity asks whether the inputs meet the standard of trusted data described above. Permitted authority asks what the agent is actually allowed to do. Runtime controls concern the checks that operate while the agent works. Financial consequence concerns what an action can do to exposure and liquidity. System-level propagation concerns what happens beyond the single institution.

The ordering isn't arbitrary. The first two dimensions sit closest to the agent: what it knows and what it may do. The last two sit furthest out: what the action costs and how it spreads. Runtime controls connect the two, operating at the moment of action, which is precisely where the paper says the sources differ in emphasis.

The paper is explicit that the framework and the hypothetical cases are the author's proposals. They're not drawn from a regulator's text, and the paper doesn't present them as such.

## A collateral workflow as test case

To show the framework in use, the paper applies it to a hypothetical ISDA/CSA collateral-management workflow, illustrating it across advisory, approval-based and bounded autonomous modes. The three modes make a sensible gradient: the same underlying task, with progressively more authority handed to the system.

The workflow was chosen because it requires reliable contractual interpretation, deterministic calculations, authorised communication and potential consequences for exposure and liquidity. Those four requirements line up with the framework's concerns. Interpretation and calculation test information integrity. Authorised communication tests permitted authority. Exposure and liquidity consequences test the financial dimension.

The paper restricts what readers should take from the exercise. It's an illustrative design exercise, not evidence that banks currently operate the described agents. Nothing in the case should be read as a description of industry practice. It's a way of showing how the five dimensions might be applied, nothing more.

## Beyond the institution: resilience and aggregation

The last two RAAF dimensions push the analysis outside any single firm, and the paper's treatment is hedged accordingly.

On exposure to the wider economy, it suggests that banks may be exposed to enterprises disrupted by AI even if those banks deploy very little AI themselves. A bank that never builds an agent could still carry the consequences of its customers' AI-driven disruption. In response, the paper proposes that existing risk processes could incorporate forward-looking sector assessment, business-model reviews, stress scenarios and concentration analysis. That's framed as a possibility, building on existing processes rather than inventing new ones.

On correlated behaviour, the paper's caution is that the aggregate effect of common agent behaviour may not be captured by testing one institution or one model at a time. This returns to the earlier observation that a transaction can be individually valid yet collectively destabilising. If supervision examines firms in isolation, it may not see a pattern that only appears when many firms act alike. The claim is hedged, and I'd keep it that way: the paper raises a gap in the testing approach rather than demonstrating a failure.

## What the paper can and can't tell you

A framework paper earns trust partly by marking its own boundaries, and this one does so repeatedly. It describes itself as a working paper presenting conceptual analysis, and it isn't legal advice. Its method is documentary comparison rather than causal evaluation. Its case is a design exercise. And it acknowledges the absence of empirical validation.

For technical readers and founders, the limits shape how to use it. The RAAF is a vocabulary for structuring supervisory and design questions, not a tested scoring system. The paper sets those limits itself: its collateral case isn't evidence that banks currently operate the described agents, the RAAF has no empirically validated thresholds, predictive performance or tested causal effects, and empirical testing with bank data would be required before claiming that it improves incident rates or financial outcomes. Those are open points, and the paper doesn't claim otherwise.

What the paper does offer is a clear argument about where attention should go. Accuracy testing of a model is insufficient when its outputs become instructions. "Agentic" is too coarse a label to assess risk. Clean data doesn't prevent an overbroad permission, and a sound individual transaction doesn't prevent a collective problem. Stated that plainly, the argument is hard to dismiss, even without empirical support.

The open question the paper leaves is the one it names itself: what evidence would show that controls over data and authority remain effective when agents transact at speed and scale? Until someone supplies a way to gather it, whether through supervisory testing that looks across firms rather than at one at a time or through some other route, the RAAF remains a well-organised set of questions waiting for its first real test.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the Risk-Adjusted Autonomy Framework?**
The RAAF is a framework proposed by the author of the paper for supervising AI agents in banking. It has five analytical dimensions: information integrity, permitted authority, runtime controls, financial consequence and system-level propagation. The paper offers it as a supervisory and managerial decision aid, not an existing standard or automated scoring methodology.

**Why does the paper treat executing a transaction differently from recommending one?**
Execution can create legal obligations, change market exposures, transfer assets, trigger liquidity demands and compress the time available to detect errors. For that reason the paper argues that the unit of supervision can be the end-to-end decision-and-action chain, not the model alone.

**Which jurisdictions does the paper compare?**
It compares selected instruments from the United Kingdom, Singapore and the European Union, with BIS analysis treated as an international source of supervisory insight rather than a fourth jurisdiction. It finds considerable convergence on accountability and risk-based oversight, but differing emphases on the moment of action, cross-framework coordination and the aggregation of correlated exposures.

**Is good data enough to make an AI agent safe?**
No. The paper says data trust is necessary but not sufficient: a well-governed dataset can support an unauthorised action, and a model with reliable inputs can still have an overbroad permission scope. A transaction can also be individually valid yet collectively destabilising when many firms take similar actions.

**Has the framework been tested on real bank data?**
No. The paper acknowledges the absence of empirical validation, and its ISDA/CSA collateral-management case is a hypothetical design exercise. It states that empirical testing with bank data would be required before claiming that the RAAF improves incident rates or financial outcomes.
