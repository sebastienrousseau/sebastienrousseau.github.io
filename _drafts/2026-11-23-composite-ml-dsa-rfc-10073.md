# Composite ML-DSA: A Post-Quantum Hybrid as One Atomic Algorithm

**The strongest idea in this Standards Track RFC is that a post-quantum hybrid doesn't have to change your protocol: by presenting one public key and one signature value, Composite ML-DSA lets existing cryptographic agility mechanisms do the work, while a deliberately large menu of combinations serves regulators rather than implementers.**

## Why a hybrid signature at all

The document is RFC 10073, *Composite Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (ML-DSA) for Use in X.509 Public Key Infrastructure*, published by the IETF in November 2026 and written by M. Ounsworth, J. Gray, M. Pala, J. Klaußner and S. Fluhrer.

The RFC opens from a premise it states without qualification: traditional signature algorithms such as RSA, DSA and their elliptic curve variants will become vulnerable to quantum attacks. It frames the situation as one of timing alone. Traditional algorithms, it says, will be broken in the future but will remain strong in the interim, and the only uncertainty is when.

That framing matters because it rules out the lazy reading of the problem. If the break is certain and only the date is unknown, then waiting isn't a neutral choice. But the document is just as frank about the cost of moving early. Aggressive migration timelines may require deploying post-quantum cryptography (PQC) algorithms before their implementations have been fully hardened or certified. The RFC hedges this, and so should we: it says "may", not "will".

Put those two pressures side by side and the case for a hybrid emerges. You want protection against the quantum future, but the new algorithms and their implementations are young. The RFC says that dual-algorithm data protection may be desirable over a longer time period, to hedge against security vulnerabilities and other implementation flaws in the new implementations. Again the language is conditional. The document suggests a rationale; it doesn't claim to have proved the hybrid is always the right call.

It's worth being clear about what a composite buys you. Composite algorithms retain some security even if one of their component algorithms is broken. The phrase is "some security", not full security, and the RFC doesn't oversell it. The practical reading is that a failure in one component doesn't automatically leave you with nothing.

## The document and its authors

Before turning to mechanics, the provenance is worth a paragraph, because founders and engineers weighing a standard should know what kind of standard it is. The RFC sits in the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) stream and its category is Standards Track. It was published in November 2026 and carries ISSN 2070-1721. The boilerplate describes it as an Internet Standards Track document: a product of the IETF that represents the consensus of the IETF community and has been approved for publication by the IESG.

The author list spans four organisations. M. Ounsworth and J. Gray are listed from Entrust, M. Pala from OpenCA Labs, J. Klaußner from Bundesdruckerei GmbH and S. Fluhrer from Cisco Systems. I won't read more into that composition than the document supports. What the list does show is that this isn't a single-vendor artefact.

The document also tells readers how to parse its normative language. The key words such as "MUST" and "SHOULD" are to be interpreted as described in BCP 14 (RFC 2119 and RFC 8174) when, and only when, they appear in all capitals. That's standard practice, but it's the reason a lower-case "should" in this specification carries no special force. The specification is also consistent with the terminology defined in RFC 9794.

## One public key, one signature value

The central design decision is easy to state. The composite algorithm presents a single public key and signature value, so it can be treated as a single atomic algorithm at the protocol level.

This is a bolder choice than it first appears, and I think it's the right one. The RFC explains the contrast directly: many PQ/T hybrids require modification of the protocol to make it "hybrid aware", whereas this specification presents the hybrid as a standalone algorithm and can therefore take advantage of existing cryptographic agility mechanisms.

Consider what that means for anyone who has to ship. A hybrid that the protocol must understand is a hybrid that touches every protocol that wants to use it. A hybrid that looks like any other algorithm is one that slots into whatever negotiation or algorithm-identification machinery already exists. The RFC defines "algorithm" in this context as generally referring to any function that has a registered Object Identifier (OID) for use within an ASN.1 AlgorithmIdentifier. A composite gets its own identity in that scheme, and the surrounding system treats it as it would any other.

There's a related vocabulary point that clarifies what the RFC is and isn't claiming. It defines Protocol Backwards Compatibility as a property whereby a new feature can be added to a protocol without requiring any changes to the protocol's specification and only minimal changes to its implementations. The atomic-algorithm approach is aimed squarely at that kind of low-friction addition. The RFC frames this as design intent: protocols such as PKCS #10, CMP, X.509, CMS and the Trust Anchor Format should be able to treat composite algorithms as they would any other algorithm, without the protocol layer needing any "hybrid awareness".

## What goes into the composite

The post-quantum half is ML-DSA, the Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm defined in FIPS 204. The specification uses it as the Post-Quantum component of its PQ/T hybrids. The traditional half is drawn from a defined set. Instantiations of the Composite ML-DSA scheme are provided based on ML-DSA, RSASSA-PSS, RSASSA-PKCS1-v1_5, ECDSA, Ed25519 and Ed448.

The terminology section pins down each traditional component. RSA appears as the Probabilistic Signature Scheme, RSASSA-PSS, defined in RFC 8017. ECDSA is the Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm defined in FIPS 186-5. EdDSA is the Edwards-curve Digital Signature Algorithm defined in RFC 8410, with two parameter sets: Ed25519 and Ed448. Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) is used as defined in RFC 5280, and DER means Distinguished Encoding Rules as defined in X.690.

The document offers a small illustration of what counts as a composite cryptographic element. Its examples are asymmetric algorithms such as "ML-DSA-65" or "RSASSA-PSS". And "signature" is used broadly: a digital cryptographic signature, making no assumptions about which algorithm.

This article doesn't go into the internal construction of the composite signature, which the RFC specifies in its own sections on key generation, signing, verification and serialisation. What matters here is that the externally visible shape, one key and one signature, is the property the rest of the design depends on.

## Why there are so many combinations

If you scan the list of component algorithms and wonder why a specification would define so many pairings, the RFC answers plainly. Serving the regulatory re-certification use case, more than any other design requirement, is what motivates the large number of algorithm combinations in this specification. It adds that the combinations are tailored to meet regulatory guidelines in certain regions.

The reasoning behind the use case is hedged but concrete. In some situations, it might be easier to re-certify an existing product by adding Post-Quantum via a PQ/T hybrid to an already compliant solution without changing the traditional component, than by replacing the traditional component. In other words, a vendor with a certified traditional algorithm may prefer to leave it untouched and bolt the post-quantum half on alongside it. That preference only works if the specification offers a pairing for whichever traditional algorithm the product already uses. Hence the breadth.

This is a design stance I find persuasive, and it's a useful corrective to the instinct that a standard should be minimal. A short list would be tidier, but it would force some products to swap out a certified component, which is precisely the cost the combinations are meant to avoid. The long list is the price of meeting regulated products where they already are.

The RFC is also careful to stop that breadth from becoming a burden on readers. It is expected that organisations will choose to deploy a single composite algorithm, or a small number of them, and very few implementers will need to concern themselves with the entire list. Note the hedge: "expected". It's a forecast about deployment behaviour, not a requirement. But it tells you how to read the document. You aren't meant to implement everything; you're meant to find your pairing.

## What the specification leaves out

A good standard is clear about its edges, and this one is. Four limits stand out.

First, the specification does not specify any mandatory-to-implement algorithms. Taken together with the expectation that organisations pick one or a few composites, this means interoperability between two parties depends on them having chosen compatible combinations. The RFC doesn't designate a default that everyone must support, and it warns that the number of combinations can fracture the ecosystem into non-interoperable subgroups when different communities choose non-overlapping subsets. It encourages organisations in specific application domains to define profiles that select a small number of composites. I'd flag that as the single most practical consequence for a team planning a rollout: you'll need to agree your pairing with your counterparties rather than assume it.

Second, backwards compatibility in the sense of upgraded systems continuing to interoperate with legacy systems is not provided. This is worth keeping distinct from the protocol-level point above. The RFC defines Application Backwards Compatibility as a property indicating whether an upgraded and non-upgraded application can successfully establish communication. Section 10.3 is explicit: the mechanisms in the document do not provide application backwards compatibility, only upgraded systems will understand the OIDs it defines, and additional mechanisms will be needed where that property is required. The atomic-algorithm design helps with protocol changes, but it doesn't claim to settle whether an upgraded application can talk to one that hasn't been upgraded.

Third, there's a security-property limit. Composite ML-DSA is applicable where it is acceptable to have a signature primitive with the weaker EUF-CMA property instead of the stronger Strong Unforgeability under Chosen Message Attack (SUF-CMA) property, which Composite ML-DSA does not offer. If your application depends on SUF-CMA, this scheme isn't a fit as specified. That's a clean, checkable boundary, and a founder evaluating the scheme should test their own requirements against it before going further.

Fourth, and more modestly, the document admits that the timeline pressure itself carries risk, as discussed earlier: PQC implementations may be deployed before they've been fully hardened or certified. The composite is a mitigation for that risk, not an elimination of it.

## Reading the notation

Implementers will spend time with the algorithm descriptions, so a note on their conventions is useful. The algorithm descriptions use Python-like syntax. The symbol || represents concatenation of two byte arrays, and the notation [:] represents byte array slicing.

For functions that return several values, the document uses (a, b) to represent a pair of values, typically indicating that a function returns multiple values, with the exact conveyance mechanism (tuple, struct, output parameters, etc.) left to the implementer. That last clause is a deliberate freedom: the specification cares about what is returned, not how your language packages it. The notation (a, _) represents a pair where one value, the second in this case, is ignored.

Function signatures follow a similar pattern. The notation func(a) -> b represents a function named func that takes a as input and produces b. And Func<TYPE>() represents a function parameterised by <TYPE>, meaning its implementation will have minor differences depending on the underlying TYPE. This last convention is what lets one description cover several underlying algorithms without being repeated for each, which fits a specification that spans RSA, ECDSA and EdDSA variants alongside ML-DSA.

None of this is glamorous, but it's the part of the document you'll actually have open when writing code, and the decision to leave the multiple-return mechanism to the implementer is a sensible concession to the variety of languages in use.

## Where this leaves a team planning a migration

Pulling these pieces into a decision frame, the RFC gives you three useful things and asks you to supply two.

What it gives you is a rationale, a shape and a boundary. The rationale is the pair of pressures already described: traditional algorithms will fall to quantum attacks at an uncertain time, while new implementations may not yet be fully hardened or certified. The shape is the atomic composite, with one public key and one signature value, which retains some security if a component breaks. The boundary is the list of limits above: no mandatory-to-implement algorithms, no direct backwards-compatibility coverage, and EUF-CMA rather than SUF-CMA.

What it asks you to supply is a choice of combination and an interoperability agreement. Because the specification names no mandatory-to-implement algorithms, picking the pairing is your responsibility, though Section 10.4 offers a short list of recommendations for common use cases. If your product is already certified with a particular traditional algorithm, the re-certification logic points towards the combination that leaves that component alone. If you're building something new with no regulatory requirements or legacy implementations to consider, the RFC recommends focusing implementation effort on id-MLDSA65-ECDSA-P256-SHA512, which it says provides the best overall balance of performance and security.

There's a reasonable argument that this division of labour is the correct one for a standard at this stage. Mandating one combination would have served some deployments and stranded others, particularly those bound by regional regulatory guidelines. Declining to mandate keeps the specification usable across those regions, at the cost of pushing a coordination step onto adopters. The RFC chooses flexibility and tells you so.

I'd also push back gently on one tempting misreading. The statement that very few implementers will need to concern themselves with the entire list can sound like permission to ignore the specification's breadth. It isn't. It's a statement about who bears the cost of that breadth: the specification absorbs it so that most deployments don't have to. If you're a library author or a vendor serving regulated customers, the breadth may well be your problem; if you're a single organisation picking one composite, it largely isn't. The RFC hedges the expectation, and I'd treat it as a forecast rather than a guarantee.

## The questions the specification leaves open

Two threads in the document are worth watching because the specification itself flags them without resolving them.

The first is the length of the transition. The RFC says dual-algorithm data protection may be desirable over a longer time period, and that the migration timelines may be aggressive. Those two statements pull against each other in an interesting way: a fast move to PQC argues for deploying before hardening is complete, while a longer hedging period argues for keeping the traditional half in place well after that. How long organisations actually keep both components is a question the document raises by implication and doesn't answer.

The second is backwards compatibility. The RFC states that its mechanisms explicitly do not provide application backwards compatibility and that additional mechanisms will be needed where it is required. It lists the range of protocols in which migration and interoperability need thinking through, from TLS 1.3 and IKEv2 to S/MIME, eIDAS document signing and code signing, but it leaves the design of those additional mechanisms to the protocols and applications themselves.

For teams deciding where to spend effort first, that second question looks like the better place to start: not which of the combinations to adopt, but how an upgraded application will behave when the party on the other end hasn't moved yet.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is Composite ML-DSA?**
Composite ML-DSA is a PQ/T hybrid signature scheme defined in RFC 10073. It combines ML-DSA, defined in FIPS 204, with a traditional algorithm such as RSASSA-PSS, RSASSA-PKCS1-v1_5, ECDSA, Ed25519 or Ed448. The pair presents a single public key and signature value, so protocols can treat it as one atomic algorithm.
**Why does the RFC define so many algorithm combinations?**
The RFC says serving the regulatory re-certification use case, more than any other design requirement, motivates the large number of combinations. A vendor may find it easier to re-certify a product by adding a post-quantum component alongside an already compliant traditional one than by replacing it. Organisations are expected to deploy one composite algorithm or a small number of them.
**Which composite algorithm should a new deployment start with?**
The RFC names no mandatory-to-implement algorithm. For applications with no regulatory requirements or legacy implementations to consider, Section 10.4 recommends focusing implementation effort on id-MLDSA65-ECDSA-P256-SHA512. It also gives recommendations for applications that require RSA, are performance and bandwidth sensitive, or only allow NIST PQC Level 5.
**Does Composite ML-DSA keep upgraded systems working with legacy ones?**
No. The RFC states that its mechanisms explicitly do not provide application backwards compatibility and that only upgraded systems will understand the OIDs it defines. Where that property is required, additional mechanisms will be needed.
**What security property does Composite ML-DSA not offer?**
It does not offer Strong Unforgeability under Chosen Message Attack (SUF-CMA), only the weaker EUF-CMA property. The RFC says applications where SUF-CMA security is critical should not use it, and that it is NOT RECOMMENDED where it has not been shown that EUF-CMA is acceptable.
