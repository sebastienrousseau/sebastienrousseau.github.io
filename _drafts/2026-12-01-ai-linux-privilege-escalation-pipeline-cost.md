# 948 Linux Privilege Escalations for $1.2M: What the AI Pipeline Cost

**An automated pipeline powered by an upcoming cyber model validated and generated patches for 948 local privilege escalation vulnerabilities in Linux in a single run, at an API cost of $1.2 million. The striking number isn't the discovery count. It's that discovery stopped being the hard part.**

## A pipeline, not a single prompt

OpenAI describes the work in *A thousand fewer paths to root*, a report published on 4 November 2026. The headline figure is easy to misread as a model finding bugs. What the authors describe is a system. Their vulnerability research prototype is a pipeline of five stages that uses an upcoming cyber model at its core, covering vulnerability discovery, dynamic validation, exploitability assessment and patch generation, with a human review at the end.

The sequence is specific. First it identifies security-relevant parts of the Linux kernel. Then it statically scans those audit targets for candidate findings. It dynamically reproduces the findings in an isolated environment, creates a proof-of-concept exploit to demonstrate exploitability, generates patches, and finally a human security researcher reviews the results. The prototype was built to run inside a controlled research environment, and that framing matters for everything that follows.

What comes out of the far end is a bundle: a root cause analysis, a minimal crashing proof-of-concept, a candidate patch and a full exploit. That bundle is the unit of work. A maintainer who receives a bare crash report has to do the reproduction, the analysis and the fix themselves. A maintainer who receives all four has been handed most of the labour.

The target is a demanding one. The Linux kernel has more than 40 million lines of code, and it exposes a broad attack surface through system calls, networking, filesystems and device drivers. Nobody audits that by reading it end to end, which is why the first stage exists at all.

## Narrowing 40 million lines to 2.8 million

The pipeline didn't scan everything. It used a team of Scout agents to identify high-value audit targets, which were then passed to Analyst agents for vulnerability scanning. Scouts decide what to audit based on reachability, meaning code reachable by low-privileged users, and on security relevance.

The numbers show how much this narrows the problem. The Scout stage identified 5,034 audit targets, about 2.8 million lines of code, roughly 7% of the whole Linux kernel code base. The authors note that this significantly reduces the cost of the security audit compared with a full codebase analysis. The Scout step itself cost $3,426 in API credits, a rounding error next to the total.

I'd argue this is the least glamorous and most transferable idea in the write-up. Choosing where to look is cheap. Looking everywhere is expensive. Anyone building a similar system for a different code base, whatever its size, can borrow the principle of spending a small amount first on deciding what's worth the expensive attention.

Then the analysts went to work. Within 12 hours, the analyst agents produced 2,308 candidate findings after deduplication. That stage cost $316,549 in API credits, roughly a quarter of the final bill. The remainder went to the downstream work: reproduction, exploit construction and patching.

## Why discovery wasn't the problem

You might expect the difficulty to lie in finding the bugs. According to the authors, it didn't. The initial vulnerability discovery stage produced thousands of candidate findings, too many for security researchers to manually review and triage. The operational challenge was not discovery, but determining which findings could be reproduced, what security boundary they crossed, and which warranted the earliest maintainer attention.

That reframing has consequences for how founders should think about this class of tool. A scanner that emits thousands of unverified findings doesn't save a security team time; it hands them a queue they can't clear. The value in this pipeline sits in the validation stages, which convert a pile of suspicions into evidence.

Validation was deliberately strict. It focused on findings that were reachable by a low-privileged user, required no special capabilities, and produced measurable evidence of memory corruption. For each candidate, a triage agent generated a proof of concept and ran it in an isolated virtual machine running a Linux kernel with Kernel Address Sanitizer (KASAN). In the dynamic validation stage, agents attempt to reproduce each candidate in an isolated environment and preserve runtime evidence.

The evidence requirement is what makes the later numbers meaningful. Of 2,308 candidates, 1,724 were dynamically reproduced. A candidate that can't be made to crash a KASAN-instrumented kernel doesn't get promoted.

After reproduction comes the exploitability stage, where agents test whether validated vulnerabilities could enable information disclosure or local privilege escalation. The final result of 948 LPE exploits is therefore the product of successive filters, each demanding more proof than the last.

## The cost, stage by stage

The authors report API costs for each stage to help researchers assess the resources needed to reproduce and extend the work. The figures quoted in the report are $3,426 for Scouting, $316,549 for the analyst stage, and $1,213,430 for the total run. Divide by the 948 exploits and you get $1,279 per LPE exploit.

Read that last figure carefully, because the authors are. They focused on methodology and measuring model capability in this research, not cost. They also expect that sufficient harness engineering could meaningfully reduce the cost at each stage, so the costs they report are more an upper bound. That's a hedged expectation rather than a measured result, and it should be treated as one. Nobody has yet shown the cheaper version.

Still, even as an upper bound, $1,279 per working local privilege escalation exploit is a number that changes planning assumptions. Defenders have long relied on the idea that producing a reliable exploit is slow, scarce, skilled work. This run puts a price on it that a funded team could pay many times over.

## Testing against kernelCTF

A fair objection is that a research environment flatters the results. The authors addressed part of that. The target virtual machines ran a stock desktop operating system, not a weakened target, under the same threat model used by cybersecurity competitions.

They also ran the pipeline against an adapted kernelCTF environment with reduced attack surface. There, it obtained 25 information leak PoCs and 21 LPE exploits. At the time of writing, 26 confirmed 0-day LPE exploits have been accepted by kernelCTF in 2026. That campaign cost roughly $32,936 in API credits.

The bounty arithmetic is the part to handle with care. If the 21 vulnerabilities were accepted by the program, it would lead to around a $1.5M bounty award, roughly 45x the scanning cost. That's conditional. It describes what would happen on acceptance, and acceptance hasn't been reported for those 21. Treat the 45x figure as an illustration of the economics the authors see, not as revenue anyone has banked.

Even so, the direction of the incentive is plain. When scanning costs tens of thousands of dollars and a successful outcome pays in the low millions, the economics favour whoever runs the scan first. That's an argument for defenders to run it before anyone else does.

## Novel techniques, with a caveat

The agents didn't merely rediscover known patterns. To the best of the authors' knowledge, the agents in this pipeline discovered and used at least 10 novel Linux exploitation techniques. The hedge in that sentence is the authors' own, and I'd keep it. Novelty is hard to prove, because it means proving a negative about everything published and everything privately known.

What can be said without hedging is a comparison between model generations. The analysis pipeline using the newer cyber model in mid-October discovered more than five times as many novel LPEs as a GPT-5.6 Sol run in July, despite three months of hardening between the scans. And in June, GPT-5.5 Cyber had identified Linux kernel vulnerabilities and developed 24 local privilege escalation exploits to validate and report them.

Set those side by side. Twenty-four exploits in June. Then 948 in a single run on the newer model. The comparison isn't perfectly controlled, since the pipeline and the model both changed, and I won't claim the model alone accounts for the difference. But the trajectory over a few months is steep, and the hardening that happened in between didn't flatten it.

## The defenders are already behind the queue

While the scan was running, the wider ecosystem was moving. Within 10 days, over 100 Linux LPEs had been patched or had staging patches from other contributors, with around 300 more being publicly discussed. So the same class of bugs was being found, fixed and argued about by others in parallel.

That observation cuts two ways. It shows maintainers and contributors responding quickly. It also shows how crowded this territory has become: a vulnerability one team finds today may already be in someone else's staging tree, or in someone else's exploit.

The authors draw a conclusion about large projects from this: large, complex software projects likely require multiple rounds of scanning and fixing vulnerabilities to "bottom out" latent bugs. Note the hedge. It's an expectation, grounded in the experience of this run, not a demonstrated endpoint. One round found hundreds of exploitable flaws. Whether a second and third round converge, and how quickly, is something the evidence here doesn't settle.

## Patching has to keep pace

The authors state their belief plainly: given the rate of vulnerability discovery by frontier models, automated patch generation is essential to secure software at the pace required, and to limit the exploitation window. This is why the pipeline generates patches rather than stopping at exploits.

They're careful about what they claim for those patches. With human expert-guided iteration, they believe AI-generated patches can be functionally correct, aligned with existing coding styles, and robust to attack variants. That's a belief, not a benchmark result, and it's conditional on a human guiding the iteration. The report itself describes AI-generated patching as an important area for further research.

I'd take a side here. A pipeline that produces 948 exploits and no fixes would be a liability, however good the engineering. The patch stage and the human review stage, where security researchers review the exploit, patch and proposed severity before reporting to the maintainers, are what make the work defensible. The human step also addresses the triage problem from earlier: a person decides what reaches a maintainer's inbox.

## What this means for system operators

The most direct operational claim is also the bluntest. Having low-privileged account access on an operating system not up to date on its latest available patches should be considered functionally equivalent to root access for a well-resourced threat actor, in the near and medium term. The authors hedge it, but the hedge is narrow. It applies to unpatched systems and well-resourced attackers.

The practical corollary is about patch latency. If an unpatched kernel and a root shell are equivalent from an attacker's point of view, then the gap between a fix being published and being deployed is the exposure window. Founders running multi-tenant infrastructure, shared build runners or anything that gives untrusted users a shell should read that sentence twice.

The authors also say that defence in depth matters. Using sandboxes and isolation approaches like Firecracker and gVisor is important for limiting the blast radius of exploited vulnerabilities. The logic follows from the rest: if some local privilege escalation will always exist somewhere in a 40-million-line kernel, containment matters as much as prevention.

## Looking ahead, with appropriate scepticism

The forward-looking claims are explicitly speculative. The authors believe that within the next few years frontier models will likely be able to discover vulnerabilities in a given software project, and even less capable models may be able to develop exploits once disclosed. That's a forecast, and it deserves to be read as one. If it holds, the advantage of being first to scan erodes, because the capability spreads to cheaper models.

They name three research directions as promising: training models to write secure code by default, minimising attack surface by removing unnecessary features, and formal verification. None of these is demonstrated in the report. They're proposals, and they sit on the prevention side of a problem that the pipeline mostly attacks from the detection side.

One caution about reading the whole study. The costs are an upper bound by the authors' own account, the 45x bounty figure is conditional, the novelty claim is to the best of their knowledge, and the patch-quality claims depend on human iteration. What survives those qualifications is still substantial: a measured count of 948 LPE exploits, a measured cost of $1,213,430, and a measured finding that the bottleneck moved from finding flaws to triaging them.

The open question the authors leave is how many rounds it takes. If large projects need repeated scan-and-fix cycles to bottom out latent bugs, the next result worth watching is a second pass over the same kernel: how many of the 948 are fixed, how many new ones appear, and whether the curve from 24 to 948 bends downward once the cheap-to-find flaws are gone.
