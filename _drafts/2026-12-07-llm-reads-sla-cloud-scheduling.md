# Letting an LLM Read the SLA Without Slowing the Cloud Scheduler

**A paper in press at Scientific Reports lets a large language model turn contract language and cluster telemetry into fuzzy rules and objective weights, while fast models place each request. The simulated results are strong; the ablation that credits the LLM needs a careful read.**

> **Executive Summary**
>
> - Vakilifard, Sahafi and Broumandnia frame cloud resource allocation as a constrained optimisation problem with five conflicting objectives: utilisation, cloud service provider (CSP) net profit, customer satisfaction, scheduling latency and load balance across physical machines.
> - They argue that population-based metaheuristics hit superlinear computational bottlenecks, while numerical ML and deep reinforcement learning schedulers cannot interpret high-level SLAs.
> - GDI pairs an asynchronous LLM (gpt-4o-2024-08-06), which emits validated fuzzy membership boundaries and objective weights, with fast tactical engines that score candidate machines in real time.
> - On 1,000 simulated machines and 100,000 Google trace requests, FRA-RF reports 97.1% acceptance, 81.5% average utilisation and a load standard deviation of 7.5; letting the LLM switch between engines at runtime reports 97.4%.
> - The paper's own ablation table shows the LLM-distilled rules ahead of every alternative it tested, by 3.7 points of acceptance over grid-searched parameters and 7.9 points over hand-written expert rules.

## Five objectives pulling in different directions

Cloud resource allocation looks like bin packing, and the paper treats it as something harder: a multidimensional, constrained optimisation problem. A scheduler has to allocate heterogeneous physical resources (CPU cores, RAM and storage) to dynamic, unpredictable request streams, and "optimal" means balancing several goals at once.

The paper names five: maximising physical machine utilisation, maximising CSP profit after operational costs, maintaining load balance to prevent thermal hotspots, meeting customer SLAs, and keeping scheduling latency low enough to prevent request queues building up. It formalises them with binary decision variables, capacity constraints on each of the three resource dimensions, five objective functions and event-driven task arrivals and departures. The objectives are then folded into a single weighted score, and those weights are what the language model later adjusts.

Two modelling choices matter for what follows. Tasks have lifecycles: they take resources on arrival and release them back to the host when they expire, so the scheduler works against a moving picture rather than a static snapshot. And profit is computed from assumed prices ($0.045 per vCPU-hour, $0.0055 per GB-hour of RAM, $0.0001 per GB-hour of disk) minus energy cost from a linear power model, so the dollar figures later in the paper are outputs of those assumptions, not of a real bill.

The paper also argues that hyperscale cloud environments differ from the hardware scheduling problems they superficially resemble, because they bring tenant-driven arrival burstiness, multi-tenant SLA contracts and variable economic utility models.

## Why the existing toolkit falls short, according to the paper

The related-work section sorts prior approaches into families, each with a characteristic weakness.

Deterministic heuristics such as First Fit Decreasing (FFD) and Best Fit Decreasing (BFD) offer low computational overhead but can lead to resource fragmentation under multi-resource constraints. When requests use CPU, RAM and storage in different proportions, a rule that packs one dimension tightly can strand capacity in another.

Metaheuristics attack the multi-objective problem more directly. The paper cites a genetic-based placement algorithm by Abohamama and Hamouda aimed at energy consumption and SLA violations, and Chakraborty et al.'s combination of Harris Hawks Optimisation with Genetic Algorithms (HHO-GA). The cost is computational: population-based methods need iterative fitness evaluations over large candidate spaces, which the authors say introduces superlinear overhead as datacentre size grows and makes them hard to deploy for sub-second scheduling. A scheduler that breaches the SLA while computing a better placement for it hasn't solved much.

Learning-based schedulers fixed the latency problem. Predictive regression and tree ensembles have been used to forecast server load, and Deep Reinforcement Learning techniques such as Deep Q-Networks (DQN) and Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) have been applied to container scheduling. The paper's criticism is that these operate as numerical black boxes: they cannot interpret the operational intent expressed in high-level SLAs, and they often struggle to adapt to out-of-distribution workload shifts without complete retraining.

Fuzzy logic has been tried as a bridge, for example Thapliyal and Dimri's FL-BFD, which combines fuzzy scoring with Best Fit Decreasing. The paper's objection is that standard fuzzy schedulers rely on static, human-engineered membership functions, and that neuro-fuzzy variants such as ANFIS can tune parameters but still cannot read a natural-language directive. That is the gap GDI is designed to close.

## Two layers, two jobs

GDI is a dual-layer neuro-symbolic architecture. The upper layer reasons about policy; the lower layer places requests.

The Strategic Reasoning Layer uses gpt-4o-2024-08-06 through a structured API. It reads natural-language SLA directives together with a telemetry vector sampled over an asynchronous sliding window: average utilisation, recent rejection ratios, cluster load standard deviation and scheduling latency. Using zero-shot Chain-of-Thought prompting, it emits a JSON payload that names which tactical engine to use, recalibrates the five objective weights, and sets new boundaries for the fuzzy membership functions. The paper's example shows the "Low" utilisation band widening from [0.00, 0.00, 0.20, 0.40] to [0.00, 0.00, 0.35, 0.55] when the directive prioritises throughput during a traffic surge.

The design detail I'd point an engineering lead at first is the validation step. Before anything reaches the cluster, the payload is parsed against a schema with Pydantic, the weights must sum to 1.0, and each membership function's parameters must be in order. If the output is malformed, breaks a constraint, or the API takes longer than 2.0 seconds, the orchestrator falls back to pre-compiled expert Mamdani rules, so the real-time layer is never left waiting on the model.

The Tactical Execution Layer then does the work that has to be fast. It includes a deterministic baseline, a fuzzy allocation engine, evolutionary variants (FRA-GA and FRA-PSO) that tune membership functions offline, an ANFIS variant, a neural network (FRA-DNN) trained by behavioural cloning on FRA-GA's allocations, and a Random Forest (FRA-RF) of 100 trees with a maximum depth of 12. For each request, the engine filters machines that have capacity and scores the candidates using the LLM-configured boundaries.

My view is that keeping the language model out of the per-request path is the design's best decision. A model call inside each placement would bring its own latency into a problem whose central difficulty is latency. The paper's complexity result is the formal version of that: because tree depth is capped, scoring one host with FRA-RF takes O(1) time, so one request costs O(N) across N machines and a stream of M requests costs O(M·N).

## What the experiments measured

The evaluation runs in three parts: a 9-machine micro-benchmark with 100 requests, a 1,000-machine run with 100,000 requests drawn from the Google Cluster Workload Traces, and an ablation plus a scaling study. The large run is a simulation: 1,000 identical machines, each with 32 cores, 256 GB of RAM and 4,000 GB of storage, fed with real trace data. The problem is formulated for heterogeneous machines, but this experiment uses a homogeneous cluster. The paper does not say which release of the Google traces it sampled; Google publishes both a 2011 and a 2019 dataset.

In that setting, FRA-RF accepted 97,100 of 100,000 requests (97.1%), reached 81.5% average utilisation, kept the standard deviation of host utilisation at 7.5 and produced $118,400 of modelled net profit. The headline numbers belong to this one engine rather than to every GDI configuration: FRA-ANFIS achieved the most even load (7.5 for FRA-RF against 6.8 for FRA-ANFIS), and FRA-DNN scored slightly higher on the paper's composite customer satisfaction metric (87.2% against 87.1%).

The paper does compare against published methods on the same workload. FL-BFD accepted 89,500 requests and HHO-GA 91,200; the paper reports that FRA-RF cut rejections by 67.0% against HHO-GA and by 72.3% against FL-BFD. HHO-GA took 132.4 seconds to schedule the trace, against 24.8 seconds for FRA-RF.

When the LLM is also allowed to switch engines at runtime (FRA-ANFIS at moderate arrival rates up to 500 requests per second, FRA-RF above 2,000, FRA-GA recalibrating in quiet periods), the paper reports 97.4% acceptance, 82.1% utilisation, $120,400 of net profit and a mean scheduling latency of 0.21 ms per request. That is a small increment on the best static engine, and it is the configuration that exercises the whole closed loop.

## The ablation is the result to read closely

An LLM-based system invites the suspicion that the language model is decoration and that the fast executors do the work. The ablation is the paper's answer. It holds FRA-RF fixed and changes only where the fuzzy rules and membership functions come from: random initialisation, hand-engineered expert rules, offline grid search over a historical trace partition, or LLM distillation at runtime.

The results, from the paper's Table 5, are 78,410 accepted requests for random rules, 89,200 for expert rules, 93,400 for grid search and 97,100 for the LLM-distilled rules. Load standard deviation falls from 16.2 to 11.8, 9.4 and 7.5 respectively.

Here the paper is inconsistent with itself. The abstract and conclusion say the LLM layer yields "an independent 3.7% acceptance gain and 20.2% lower load variance over static expert rules". The ablation text says the same two figures are "relative to the grid-searched configuration", and the table agrees with the text: 97,100 against 93,400 is 3.7 percentage points, and 7.5 against 9.4 is a 20.2% reduction in load standard deviation. Against the expert rules the gap is wider, 7.9 points of acceptance and a 36% lower standard deviation. Either way the LLM-distilled rules come out ahead in this setup; the abstract simply names the wrong comparison. I'd quote the table, not the abstract, and I'd expect the final edited version to reconcile the two.

Holding the executor constant is the right design for this question, because any difference in outcome then comes from where the fuzzy parameters came from. The grid-search comparison is also the stronger test: the paper says grid search was tuned offline on a historical partition and cannot adapt to runtime shifts, which is precisely the advantage the LLM loop claims.

## What the scaling study shows

The paper separates the two things that usually grow together. Holding the workload fixed and growing the cluster from 100 to 1,000 machines, FRA-RF's runtime rose from 11.5 to 13.8 seconds, which the authors attribute to pre-filtering machines without headroom. Holding the cluster at 1,000 machines and growing the workload from 10,000 to 100,000 requests, FRA-RF's runtime grew roughly linearly, from 2.9 to 24.8 seconds, while HHO-GA rose from 12.4 to 132.4 seconds. The FRA-RF figures are the measured counterpart to the O(N) per-request claim. The paper does not say whether LLM calls are counted in these runtimes; since the LLM runs asynchronously, I read them as the cost of the tactical layer.

## Where this leaves founders and engineers

For an engineer building scheduling infrastructure, the lesson is architectural. The paper doesn't argue that an LLM should schedule requests. It argues that an LLM should interpret intent and hand the result, in a narrow and validated format, to components that are already fast. That matches each tool to what it does well: language models for reading SLAs, tree ensembles and neural networks for quick inference, fuzzy systems for graded decisions at the boundaries.

The validation layer is the piece worth copying even if the rest isn't. A model that writes configuration into a production control loop needs a schema, invariants it cannot break, a timeout and a known-good fallback. GDI has all four, and that is what makes it plausible to put a language model anywhere near a scheduler.

Some limits are worth stating plainly. The evidence comes from simulation on a homogeneous cluster, with profit computed from assumed prices. The tables report single figures with no spread across repeated runs. The paper does not say how often the 2.0-second fallback to expert rules was triggered, or what the LLM calls cost. And it is an Article in Press: Scientific Reports says the final edited version will replace it, which matters here because the abstract and the ablation table currently disagree about the comparison. The authors list multi-region federations, carbon and energy criteria and continuous online adaptation under infrastructure failures as future work.

The structure is still persuasive. Each earlier generation failed on one axis: heuristics on fragmentation, metaheuristics on superlinear cost, ML and DRL schedulers on reading the contract. GDI assigns each axis to the component suited to it. The test that would settle the argument is a workload shift outside anything the tactical engines were trained on, run on real tenants rather than a simulator. That is where the paper says numerical models would normally need retraining, and where an asynchronous LLM re-deriving fuzzy boundaries from fresh telemetry would either earn its place or not.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is Generative Decision Intelligence (GDI)?**
It is the framework proposed by Vakilifard, Sahafi and Broumandnia in a Scientific Reports Article in Press. An asynchronous LLM reads natural-language SLAs and cluster telemetry and outputs fuzzy membership boundaries and objective weights; fast tactical engines (Random Forest, a deep neural network, ANFIS and others) use them to place each request on a physical machine.

**Does the LLM make each scheduling decision?**
No. The LLM runs asynchronously and configures the tactical layer. Per-request placement is done by the tactical engines, and if the LLM returns invalid parameters or takes longer than 2.0 seconds, the system falls back to pre-compiled expert rules.

**What results does the paper report?**
On a simulated cluster of 1,000 machines processing 100,000 requests from the Google Cluster Workload Traces, the FRA-RF engine accepted 97.1% of requests at 81.5% average utilisation with a load standard deviation of 7.5, and scheduled the trace in 24.8 seconds. With the LLM switching engines at runtime, acceptance was 97.4%.

**How much does the LLM layer itself contribute?**
In the ablation, with the Random Forest executor held constant, LLM-distilled rules accepted 97,100 requests against 93,400 for grid-searched parameters and 89,200 for hand-written expert rules. The abstract attributes the 3.7% and 20.2% figures to the expert-rule comparison, but the ablation table shows they are the gains over grid search.

**Are the results peer-settled?**
Not yet in final form. The paper is an Article in Press: it was accepted on 23 October 2026 and carries a permanent DOI, but Scientific Reports says the final edited version will replace it. The experiments are simulations, so they describe that setting rather than a production cloud.
