# Treasury Is Going Corridor by Corridor, Not One Global Model

**The DBS and EY study of 422 finance leaders points to a treasury function that can't be run from a single banking model, currency or payment rail any more. The strongest evidence is how leaders now split liquidity, FX and banking decisions by corridor, and how many already hold more than one currency.**

## Who was asked, and why the sample matters

The report is *The Great Rewiring: Money in a New Order*, a business and finance leaders' handbook published by DBS Bank in 2026, for which DBS commissioned EY to undertake the study. The research engaged 422 global business and finance leaders, spanning CFOs, finance heads and senior treasury executives. All of them sat at organisations with annual revenues above USD500 million, and the fieldwork covered 11 markets across Asia, Europe and North America.

That sample shapes how the findings should be read. These aren't start-ups with a single bank account and a single currency. They're large organisations whose treasury teams already carry the weight of multiple markets, and the study's central observation is that this weight is growing. Supply chain fragmentation, deepening market infrastructure and greater internationalisation mean organisations are simultaneously managing more markets, currencies and payment modes.

For technical readers and founders, the useful reading is this: the problems described here are what treasury looks like once a business crosses a certain size and reach. If you're building payments, treasury or reconciliation software, or preparing to expand, this is the environment your product or your finance team will operate in.

The foreword frames the shift in expectations plainly. Leaders are no longer looking to their banks simply to process transactions. They want partners that help them handle complexity, and they want visibility, connectivity and counsel. That sentence carries the rest of the study. Almost every finding below is, in some form, a request for one of those three things.

## Pressure from several directions at once

The study doesn't describe a single crisis. It describes organisations under pressure on multiple fronts: shifting trade flows, rising counterparty and sanctions risk, and growing pressure to expand into new markets. Layered on top are energy price and foreign exchange unpredictability, which complicate cash forecasting and liquidity planning, and fragmented treasury, payment and technology systems that drive manual workarounds and exception handling, raising operational risk.

Take trade flows first. Three-quarters of respondents indicate that shifting trade routes and shipping patterns have required their organisations to adapt to changing corridors. That's a large majority describing the same operational change, and it's the figure that most directly supports the article's central argument: if three in four have had to adapt to changing corridors, a single global treasury template is hard to defend.

Counterparty and sanctions risk comes next. The study describes a higher risk that business partners may be unable to fulfil their obligations, and that regulatory sanctions could disrupt or restrict transactions. Wenbai Zhu, CFO of Chery Indonesia, puts it in first-person terms: sanctions risks are one of their biggest concerns, and the company has an internal leadership group to monitor compliance with sanctions. A standing leadership group is a heavier response than a compliance checklist, and it tells you where the issue sits in that organisation's priorities.

Currency exposure is the third strand. Lijing Jiang, CFO of Hangzhou CIEC Group Co., Ltd., says that as an automotive company with a highly international supply chain, they are particularly exposed to foreign exchange movements. The study's wider finding matches the anecdote: heightened FX volatility and settlement uncertainty are increasing treasury complexity, which reinforces the need for stronger risk management and real-time visibility.

Energy is the fourth. Rising energy price volatility is emerging as a liquidity management challenge, complicating cash forecasting and increasing uncertainty around short-term funding needs. It's easy to file energy under operations rather than treasury, but the study places it squarely in the cash forecast.

## The visibility problem survives faster payments

One finding deserves more attention than it usually gets. Despite faster payment modes and automation, organisations still struggle to maintain oversight across payments, cash, exposures and liquidity. Visibility and payment friction remain key treasury issues.

It would be natural to assume that faster rails and more automation solve oversight. The study says they haven't, at least not yet. A plausible reading, though the study states only the outcome, is that speed and automation address individual payments while oversight is a question of seeing everything together. What the study does state is the mechanism on the other side of the ledger: fragmented systems and issues in payment processing force teams into manual workarounds and exception handling, raising operational risk and diverting capacity from higher-value activities.

That last clause matters for founders. The cost of fragmentation isn't only the error that slips through. It's also the skilled people who spend their days on exceptions instead of on analysis. Currency diversification adds to the strain, since it's increasing forecasting complexity and reducing visibility into payment and cash positions. Every additional currency or payment mode, in other words, makes the visibility problem harder rather than easier.

## Why corridors, not a global model

Changing trade flows and expansion into new markets push leaders to evaluate liquidity, FX and banking needs corridor by corridor rather than through one global model. This is the study's most practical shift, and it follows directly from the pressures above.

Expansion is a good example of why. Respondents are increasingly facing pressure to expand into new markets as organisations seek to diversify geographic risk. The same study notes an increased pressure to expand to new markets and to exit or deprioritise some existing ones, so the map is being redrawn in both directions. ASEAN and ANZ are emerging as preferred expansion regions.

Expansion carries its own cost. The trend is driving greater complexity, requiring support for new currencies, banking networks and regulatory environments. Each new market brings its own currency, its own banking network and its own regulator, and a global model that assumes uniformity gets stretched by every one of them.

This is where I'd take a side. The corridor view isn't a fashionable relabelling of regional treasury. It's a response to the specific combination the study documents: routes that have shifted, markets that are being added and dropped, and settlement choices that vary with volatility. A team that tries to answer all of that with one currency policy and one banking relationship is optimising for administrative tidiness at the expense of the exposures that actually hurt.

## A multi-currency, multi-instrument, multi-modal future

The study describes the future operating model as multi-currency, multi-instrument and multi-modal, with volatility now having a greater impact on the choice of settlement currency and payment modality. Currency volatility is having a greater impact on those choices, and alternative currencies and token-based settlement modes are gaining traction as operating models diversify.

The numbers show this isn't a forecast about some distant state. A majority, 55%, of respondents hold selective exposure to multiple currencies for specific corridors or markets. A further 23% already transact across multiple currencies beyond the reserve currencies. The word "selective" in the first figure is worth noticing: most organisations aren't diversifying everywhere, they're diversifying where a particular corridor calls for it. That's the corridor logic applied to currency.

Sylvia Yau, Head of Payments at SF Group, gives a concrete example of the approach. The firm is building a diversified, multi-currency settlement framework centred on local currencies, particularly across emerging trade corridors such as Vietnam and other ASEAN markets. She says this helps reduce unnecessary FX conversion costs and mitigate exposure to cross-border payment disruptions arising from geopolitical tensions. Two benefits are named, lower conversion cost and lower exposure to disruption, and both are tied to a specific set of corridors rather than to a global policy.

Token-based or blockchain-enabled modalities are expected to grow. That's a hedged expectation rather than a measured result, and it should be read as such. The study is more direct about tokenised money later, but for settlement modalities generally it points to expected growth, not accomplished fact.

## RMB: adoption follows trade integration

The RMB section is the clearest case study of the corridor argument, because the adoption pattern tracks geography and trade ties rather than spreading evenly. RMB is gaining increased acceptance as a trade settlement currency. Adoption is higher across markets with deep trade integration and geographic proximity to China, as highlighted by respondents in Taiwan, Hong Kong, South Korea, Indonesia and Singapore.

The study also identifies where the next movement may come from. The next wave sits in the markets where intent outpaces use, which the study names as Australia and the UK. A gap between intention and use is a useful thing to find in a survey, since it marks where the infrastructure, pricing or counsel hasn't yet caught up with what leaders say they want.

Two further points complete the picture. RMB adoption remains anchored in trade and investment, so it's tied to commercial flows rather than being a free-floating preference. And recent RMB appreciation has made settlement in RMB more attractive to Chinese exporters, who are increasingly offering to redenominate on better commercial terms. That's a mechanism rather than a statistic: when the currency appreciates, exporters have a reason to prefer it, and buyers can be offered better commercial terms for agreeing to it. For a finance team that buys from China, the practical takeaway is that settlement currency has become something to negotiate rather than something to assume.

## Tokenised money and AI: imminent, and practical

Two technology themes run through the study, and the way it describes them is more measured than the usual hype.

On tokenised money, the study says adoption is now imminent, with leaders evaluating, piloting or deploying use cases and most expecting benefits within two years. Notice the range of stages in that sentence. Some leaders are still evaluating, some are piloting and some are deploying. The expectation of benefits within two years is held by most, not all, and it's an expectation rather than a result, so treat it as a signal of direction.

On AI, the study says it's moving from experimentation to execution, with value concentrated in practical, workflow-level use cases like payment investigations, cash forecasting and reconciliation. Those three examples are unglamorous, and that's the point. They're the repetitive, exception-heavy tasks that the earlier sections identified as draining treasury capacity. Payment investigations and reconciliation sit exactly where manual workarounds and exception handling pile up, and cash forecasting is where energy and FX unpredictability hurt most.

The barriers are described just as precisely. They cluster around trust and risk, capabilities and unproven value, rather than any doubt that the technology works. For a founder selling into treasury teams, that framing is useful. The objection you'll meet is unlikely to be "does this work?" It's more likely to be "can we trust it, do we have the people, and has anyone proved the value?" A product that answers those three questions with evidence has a better chance than one that demonstrates yet another capability.

## What leaders now ask of their banks

The study's closing theme returns to the foreword's point about banks. Organisations are turning to banks to provide ecosystem access, technology enablement and implementation support. They also increasingly expect banking partners to provide liquidity support across different markets, financing solutions and regulatory expertise across jurisdictions.

Advice is part of the request too. Leaders are looking to banks for advisory support on liquidity optimisation, risk management and handling regulatory complexity in an increasingly layered payments environment. Set that beside the pressures catalogued earlier and the demand makes sense. If a treasury team is adding corridors, currencies and payment modes faster than it can build in-house expertise for each, it needs a partner whose coverage extends across them.

The study ties this back to a challenge it expects ahead. A key future challenge will be coordinating multiple ecosystems simultaneously while maintaining visibility, control and liquidity, and building resilience, rather than managing a single banking model, currency or payment infrastructure. That is the study's own forecast of a challenge ahead, not a result measured in the survey.

## What the evidence supports, and what it leaves open

A few limits are worth stating. The sample is large, 422 leaders, but it's drawn from organisations with annual revenues above USD500 million, so it doesn't speak for smaller businesses. The study is commissioned by a bank, which is relevant when it concludes that leaders want more from their banks, though the specific figures it reports, such as the three-quarters who adapted to changing corridors and the 55% and 23% currency splits, are stated as survey results.

Some of the most forward-looking statements are expectations. Token-based or blockchain-enabled modalities are expected to grow, and most leaders expect tokenised money benefits within two years. Those are sentiments about the future, and the study can't confirm them yet.

What the evidence does support is a clear change of unit. The treasury problem is no longer "how do we run one global model well?" It's "how do we run several corridors well, each with its own currency mix, settlement choice, regulator and risk profile, while still seeing everything at once?" The 55% holding selective multi-currency exposure, the three-quarters adapting to shifting trade routes and the uneven RMB adoption all point the same way.

The question the study leaves open is the one it names as the key future challenge: whether organisations can coordinate multiple ecosystems at once without losing visibility, control and liquidity. Visibility and payment friction already remain key issues despite faster payments and automation. If every new currency and modality makes forecasting harder and positions less visible, then the next test is whether the ecosystem access, technology enablement and implementation support that leaders are asking their banks for can close that gap before the number of corridors grows further.
