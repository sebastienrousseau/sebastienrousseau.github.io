# Quantum Risk Is a Programme, Not an Algorithm Swap, Say UK Regulators

**UK regulators have set out effective practices for quantum-related cyber risk, and their central message is that managing it involves more than adopting new cryptographic algorithms: it takes an inventory, a plan, accountable owners and a multi-year view.**

> **Executive Summary**
>
> - The Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA), the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) and the Bank of England published the document jointly on 19 November 2026.
> - Quantum computing poses significant risks to the cryptographic methods that currently underpin cybersecurity in the financial sector.
> - Preparation spans governance, identification and planning, phased implementation, and assurance, and it may involve a complex, multi-year programme of work across the firm and its third parties.
> - The practices are not part of the regulators' formal expectations, and firms can adopt them in a manner appropriate to their size, complexity and risk profile.

## A joint publication with a deliberately limited status

Three bodies put their names to this document: the Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA), the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) and the Bank of England, collectively referred to as the regulators. It was published on 19 November 2026. The word "firm" carries the same meaning it has in the regulators' supervisory statement (SS) and Final Guidance (FG) on cyber risk and resilience management, abbreviated throughout as SS/FG, and that meaning includes financial market infrastructures (FMIs). A footnote adds that the SS/FG is itself still a draft, subject to consultation via CP17/26.

The document highlights effective practices that firms can adopt to manage risks to their cyber resilience relating to quantum computing. That sentence is easy to skim past, but the status it implies is worth dwelling on. The practices are complementary to the SS/FG, yet separate from it, and the publication is explicit that they are not part of the regulators' formal expectations.

Why would a regulator publish something it doesn't formally expect? One reading, and it's my framing rather than a claim from the document, is that it gives firms a reference point without adding a new compliance obligation. What the source does say is that the expectations in the SS/FG apply regardless of the technology firms use. Quantum doesn't suspend them. The effective practices simply address quantum-related cyber risks in greater detail.

The provenance is also worth noting. The practices build on relevant publications by the regulators, HM Treasury, the National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC), non-UK authorities, international standard-setting bodies and industry. They have also been informed by the regulators' engagement with firms. For a founder or engineering lead, that matters: this isn't a document written in isolation from what firms are actually doing.

## The threat in plain terms

The premise is stated without softening. Quantum computing poses significant risks to the cryptographic methods that currently underpin cybersecurity in the financial sector.

The mechanism is framed conditionally. A sufficiently capable quantum computer would be able to solve certain mathematical problems that classical computers cannot solve in practice. The document grants that this could create significant opportunities, including for innovation in the financial sector, but it also creates new risks. In particular, it could undermine the public-key cryptography widely used to protect financial transactions, communications, records and other data, and to support authentication and digital signatures. A quantum computer capable of doing this is known as a cryptographically relevant quantum computer, or CRQC.

At present, no CRQCs are known to exist. It would be tempting to stop reading there. The publication doesn't. It says that while there is considerable uncertainty about when CRQCs will be developed, there is a strong case for firms to start to address quantum-related cyber risks. A footnote points to a 2025 survey of quantum computing experts, published by the Global Risk Institute and Evolution Inc, which suggests a CRQC is likely to be developed in the next 15 years, with a meaningful probability that it is achieved within the next 10.

The document also says the pace of technological development in quantum computing may change unexpectedly, resulting in more capable quantum computers arriving earlier than anticipated. Its practical conclusion is that firms may consider a range of plausible timelines rather than relying on a single forecast. I'd argue that's the most useful sentence for planning purposes. A single forecast invites a single deadline, and a single deadline invites the assumption that everything before it is safe.

## Why starting late costs more than it appears

The publication ties timing to exposure. It will take time and concerted effort for firms to prepare, and the later a firm begins preparing, the greater the likelihood that its transactions, communications and assets will remain exposed when the technology matures and the quantum threat materialises. The source describes a likelihood, not a certainty.

Then there's the matter of what an attacker can do today. Threat actors may adopt a "harvest now, decrypt later" (HNDL) strategy, obtaining encrypted data now with the intention of decrypting it with quantum technology in the future. The document's example is confidential customer or transaction data intercepted today that may still be sensitive when a CRQC becomes available. The language is hedged: threat actors may. But if the strategy is adopted, the exposure begins when the data is taken, not when a CRQC arrives.

That's why the document asks firms to think about how long data needs to stay confidential. A firm may need to identify data vulnerable to HNDL attacks, taking into account three things: the sensitivity of the data, its potential value to a threat actor, and the period for which it needs to remain confidential. A record that's worthless in a year is a different problem from one that must stay private for decades.

## More than an algorithm swap

Post-quantum cryptography, or PQC, refers to cryptographic methods designed to be secure against both quantum computers and classical computers. It's tempting to read the problem as a procurement question: pick the new algorithms, swap them in, move on.

The publication rejects that reading twice. Managing these risks involves more than adopting new cryptographic algorithms, and cyber resilience against quantum risks involves more than migrating to PQC. Effective preparation, it says, involves identifying and prioritising cryptographic dependencies and vulnerabilities across technology estates, understanding which systems need to be upgraded or replaced, and managing risks arising from dependencies on third-party suppliers, service providers and other connected organisations.

Migration also involves carrying out timely remedial work without weakening operational resilience, and retaining the flexibility to respond as threats and standards evolve. Those two requirements pull against each other. Speed argues for moving quickly; resilience argues for not breaking what already works. The document's answer, set out in its later sections, is ordinary ICT change discipline: assess whether new methods or transitional arrangements weaken systems, test, and be able to reverse safely.

The scale of that work is acknowledged. Preparing to move to PQC may involve a complex, multi-year programme of work across the firm and its third parties. For a small company, "multi-year" can sound like a verdict on its budget. The proportionality clause matters here, and I'll return to it below.

## Governance: who owns the problem

The publication starts with the board and senior management, who need to consider the implications of quantum computing for their firm's cyber risk and resilience. Steps they can take include setting the programme's strategic direction, agreeing a documented migration plan with clear milestones and dates, and considering the financial and non-financial resources needed, including expertise, technology and investment over the expected duration of the transition. A plan without dates is an intention.

Effective oversight, the document says, includes clearly assigning responsibility for managing quantum-related cyber risk to individuals with appropriate authority and skills, and integrating this risk into existing governance and risk management arrangements. Two qualifiers there deserve attention. Authority means the owner can actually make or force decisions across teams. Skills means the owner understands the subject well enough to challenge a vendor's claims. The document adds that firms may support this with education and training for key staff, from the board to senior technical staff and control functions, and that relevant business functions, alongside technology and security teams, belong in identifying critical systems and planning their migration.

Firms can also support effective board oversight by providing the board with clear, accurate, useful and timely management information (MI) on their quantum readiness and progress against their migration plan. That MI may include progress on the cryptographic inventory, the identification of priority systems and services, third-party readiness, and how quantum-related and migration risks relate to the firm's risk appetite.

## Discovery: the cryptographic inventory

If governance answers who, discovery answers what. Effective practice involves a firm identifying the cryptographic methods and assets used across its cyber and information and communications technology (ICT) estate. The scope is broad. It includes cryptography used to protect data at rest and in transit, to authenticate users and systems, and to create or verify digital signatures.

The document is specific about where that cryptography lives. The list covers applications, databases, application programming interfaces, certificates, public key infrastructure (PKI), authentication mechanisms, backups, networks and operational technology. Anyone who has tried to trace where a particular certificate is used will recognise how much ground that covers. Backups and operational technology in particular are places where cryptography can sit unnoticed.

Identifying dependencies on third-party systems and services is also key. Maintaining an accurate and up-to-date inventory of these methods, assets and dependencies, which the document calls a "cryptographic inventory", can help the firm assess risks and plan its migration to PQC. "Up-to-date" is the demanding part, and the closing section makes it explicit: effective practice is likely to involve keeping the cryptographic inventory current.

My view, and again it's a view and not a claim from the source, is that this is the stage most likely to be underestimated. It's unglamorous, it produces no visible security improvement on its own, and everything downstream depends on it.

## Risk analysis: turning a list into priorities

An inventory tells a firm what it has. The next step is deciding what matters. To analyse its quantum-related cyber risks, a firm may need to determine whether, and to what extent, the methods in its cryptographic inventory are vulnerable to risks from quantum computing, including the implications for confidentiality, authentication and the integrity of digitally signed information.

Those three properties don't fail in the same way. Confidentiality connects to the HNDL concern, where data taken now might be read later. Authentication and signature integrity concern whether a party or a document can be trusted. The publication lists them side by side, which is a useful reminder that quantum risk isn't only about secrecy.

Third parties come back in here. A firm may need to assess dependencies on third parties that provide cryptographic functionality, products or services, including their readiness to support PQC and whether their migration plans and timelines are compatible with the firm's own. It's a question of alignment as much as capability. A supplier that will support PQC in five years is a problem for a firm that needs it in three.

Prioritisation follows. A firm may consider prioritising vulnerabilities for remediation or upgrade, taking into account the sensitivity and required protection lifetime of the relevant data, how critical the systems and services are, any constraints from legacy systems, and how long the changes will take to implement. The last factor is easy to forget. Something that's low in criticality but slow to change may need to start early precisely because of the lead time.

## Planning: an adaptable migration plan

From the inventory and the risk analysis, a firm may develop and implement an adaptable, risk-based, proportionate and well-prioritised migration plan, covering applications, infrastructure, networks, hardware, data and third-party dependencies. The plan may set out migration priorities, sequencing, milestones and target dates.

"Adaptable" sits alongside "milestones and target dates", and the two aren't contradictory. Dates give the plan accountability; adaptability acknowledges that the ground may move. The publication notes that a firm can consider the latest relevant guidance on migration timelines, including any published by the NCSC and the regulators, which may change in response to the evolving threat landscape. A plan that can't absorb a revised timeline will break the first time one arrives.

The plan also needs to be honest about what can't easily be moved. The firm may identify systems and assets that may not support quantum-resistant cryptography, including legacy applications, hardware, devices and operational technology, and consider whether these can be upgraded or need to be replaced, retired or otherwise managed. Where a system cannot be upgraded, the document points to a risk-based approach that weighs the potential short- and long-term consequences of leaving vulnerabilities unaddressed, in accordance with the firm's established ICT change controls.

## Implementation: phased, agile and hybrid for now

On implementation, a firm may progressively introduce quantum-resistant methods and controls across its cyber and ICT estate. The document suggests a phased approach, giving priority to data that is critical or of long-term sensitivity, and to systems and services whose disruption could have a material impact on the firm, its customers or the wider financial sector. Firms can draw on established PQC standards and relevant authoritative guidance when selecting migration approaches; the publication doesn't name particular standards.

Two further ideas shape the implementation. The first is cryptographic agility ("crypto-agility"): the capability to flexibly adapt or replace cryptographic algorithms as threats, technologies and standards evolve. The document's reason is blunt: individual PQC algorithms may no longer provide effective security as the field develops. Firms may therefore consider the potential need to change algorithms when they design, develop, buy or upgrade systems.

The second is hybrid solutions, which combine traditional algorithms with PQC algorithms and may help mitigate the risk of HNDL attacks. In line with NCSC guidance, firms may treat hybrid arrangements as an interim measure while progressing towards a PQC-only end state, and may assess the security, operational and implementation risks of using both together during the transition.

## Assurance, third parties and co-ordination

The final section is the one that makes the programme framing concrete. A firm may need to test whether quantum-resistant methods and controls operate as intended, remain effective under realistic operating conditions, and interact securely with its other systems and services. It may also test its ability to reverse changes safely and check that systems do not unintentionally revert to cryptographic methods that are vulnerable to CRQCs. That last check is a detail I'd expect many plans to miss.

Response and recovery come in too. A firm may need to consider how its arrangements would apply if cryptographic protections were compromised, or if migration activity disrupted an important business service: containing the impact, re-establishing trusted authentication, communications, digital signatures and data protections, and maintaining or recovering services within applicable impact tolerances.

On third parties, firms may seek information about the preparedness and migration plans of material third parties, and may need to review and, where appropriate, update procurement processes and contracts covering their cryptographic dependencies, their readiness for PQC, and changes to their products or services that could affect the firm. The document is unambiguous on one point: the firm remains accountable for managing quantum-related cyber risk where the relevant systems or services are provided by a third party.

Some aspects of migration may require firms, third parties and other connected organisations to act in a co-ordinated way. Where that is the case, a firm may work with third parties, counterparties, financial market infrastructures, common service providers and established industry forums. For internationally active firms, this may include considering differences in standards, technical approaches and migration timelines across jurisdictions.

## Proportionality and what it means for smaller firms

The effective practices are intended to be used proportionately. Firms can adopt them in a manner appropriate to their size, complexity and risk profile. That cuts against a reflexive reading of the multi-year programme language as applying equally to every organisation.

What proportionality does and doesn't do is worth stating carefully. The source doesn't say which steps a smaller firm may skip, and I won't invent that. A firm with a small estate and few third-party dependencies will find the inventory a smaller task than one with sprawling legacy systems, but the sequence of ownership, discovery, analysis, planning, phased implementation and testing still describes the shape of the work.

For founders, the more practical reading is probably this: the cheapest time to understand what cryptography your product depends on is before you have many customers, many integrations and many suppliers. That's an argument from my own framing, not from the document, but it follows the document's logic that later starts leave more exposure.

## What the document leaves open

The publication is careful about what it claims, and that restraint is part of its value. The regulators make no forecast of their own about when a CRQC will exist; they state there is considerable uncertainty and cite an expert survey. They don't say HNDL is happening; they say threat actors may adopt the strategy. They don't add formal expectations; they describe effective practices. And they close by calling the management of quantum-related cyber risk an ongoing activity, rather than a one-off migration exercise.

Several practical questions follow, and the document leaves the answers to firms. How should a firm weigh a range of plausible timelines when setting milestone dates? Which legacy systems will end up "otherwise managed" rather than upgraded, and for how long? And how will third-party migration timelines, which the firm doesn't control, be reconciled with its own?

The one to watch is the last. The document notes that guidance on migration timelines, including that published by the NCSC and the regulators, may change in response to the evolving threat landscape, and it suggests firms work with others to manage dependencies they cannot address alone. If those timelines shift, every firm's milestone dates and every supplier's readiness will need to be revisited together. Whether the ecosystem can move in step is the open question this publication raises, and it isn't one any single firm can answer alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Are the quantum effective practices binding on UK firms?**
No. The publication says the practices are complementary to, but separate from, the regulators' SS/FG on cyber risk and resilience management, and are not part of the regulators' formal expectations. The expectations in the SS/FG, itself still a draft subject to consultation via CP17/26, apply regardless of the technology firms use.

**Who published the document, and who does it apply to?**
It was issued jointly by the Prudential Regulation Authority, the Financial Conduct Authority and the Bank of England on 19 November 2026. "Firm" is used as in the cyber risk and resilience SS/FG and includes financial market infrastructures. Firms can adopt the practices in a manner appropriate to their size, complexity and risk profile.

**What is a cryptographic inventory?**
It is an accurate and up-to-date inventory of the cryptographic methods, assets and third-party dependencies across a firm's cyber and ICT estate, covering cryptography in applications, databases, APIs, certificates, PKI, authentication mechanisms, backups, networks and operational technology. The publication says it can help the firm assess risks and plan its migration to PQC.

**Why start now if no CRQC exists yet?**
The document gives three reasons: preparing takes time and concerted effort, so a later start raises the likelihood of exposure; threat actors may adopt a "harvest now, decrypt later" strategy; and the pace of development may change unexpectedly. It suggests firms consider a range of plausible timelines rather than a single forecast.

**What does the publication say about hybrid cryptography?**
Firms can consider hybrid solutions that combine traditional and PQC algorithms, which may help mitigate the risk of HNDL attacks. In line with NCSC guidance, firms may treat hybrid arrangements as an interim measure while progressing towards a PQC-only end state.
