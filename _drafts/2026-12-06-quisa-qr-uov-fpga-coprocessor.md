# One FPGA Coprocessor Runs QR-UOV Signatures at All Three NIST Levels

**QR-UOV, a round-3 candidate in NIST's additional signatures call, had been evaluated only in software. QUISA, an instruction-set coprocessor from Queen's University Belfast, runs key generation, signing and verification for all three NIST security levels in one fixed Artix-7 footprint.**

> **Executive Summary**
>
> - QR-UOV is a Quotient Ring Unbalanced Oil and Vinegar multivariate quadratic signature scheme that has reached round 3 of NIST's additional signatures call.
> - Before this work it had been evaluated only on software platforms. The authors state that, to the best of their knowledge, no complete QR-UOV hardware architecture existed for FPGAs or ASICs.
> - QUISA is an instruction-set coprocessor, implemented at the register-transfer level and evaluated on an Artix-7 FPGA up to post-place-and-route.
> - It combines a parallel sampling and packing unit, a dual-datapath SHAKE-128/256 wrapper, an iterative modular arithmetic unit and an LU-decomposition solver over F127.
> - At 80 MHz it uses 11,144 slices and 35,248 look-up tables; SL-V key generation, signing and verification take 30.4M, 3.8M and 2.8M clock cycles, or 380.19, 47.62 and 35.01 ms.

## Why a signature scheme without hardware numbers matters

NIST launched its post-quantum cryptography standardisation initiative in 2016. The results so far are FIPS 203 (ML-KEM, from CRYSTALS-Kyber), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA, from CRYSTALS-Dilithium) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA, from SPHINCS+), with a FALCON-based standard, FN-DSA or FIPS 206, under development. In 2025 NIST also selected HQC as an alternative key encapsulation mechanism.

Signatures did not stop there. To diversify signature constructions, NIST issued an additional call in 2022, and from 40 complete submissions, 9 were selected for the ongoing round-3 evaluation in 2026. QR-UOV is one of them.

The paper opens with the hardware pressure behind all of this. Fujitsu and RIKEN have developed 64-qubit and 256-qubit quantum processors, with a roadmap that targets a 1,000-qubit system by 2026. IBM has reported Eagle (127 qubits), Heron (133 qubits) and Nighthawk (120 qubits), with plans that include a 360-qubit processor by 2026. The authors' conclusion is a hedged one: these advances indicate that future quantum computers may provide substantial computational power. Nothing in the paper claims a particular machine will break a particular scheme, and nothing here does either.

What follows is practical. A candidate that reaches round 3 attracts scrutiny of its cost as well as its security, and among the round-3 multivariate signatures only UOV and MAYO had complete hardware implementations. QR-UOV had software figures and nothing more. Implementation cost is part of what decides whether a scheme is deployable, which is why a paper about an FPGA coprocessor deserves attention beyond the hardware community.

## What QR-UOV is, in the terms that matter for hardware

QR-UOV is a multivariate quadratic signature scheme. It uses a quotient-ring algebraic framework over a finite field to reduce the public-key size compared with conventional Unbalanced Oil and Vinegar constructions. It operates over the quotient ring Fq[x]/(f), where f is an irreducible polynomial of degree ℓ, and every parameter set in the paper uses q = 127 and ℓ = 3.

Two details drive the hardware design. The first is the field: arithmetic is over the prime field with q = 127. The second is the modulus itself. Because 127 is a Mersenne prime (2^7 - 1), modular reduction can be done with shift-add folding rather than general modular arithmetic.

Smaller does not mean small. The specification's public keys are 24,255 bytes at SL-I, 71,891 bytes at SL-III and 173,676 bytes at SL-V, against signatures of 200, 292 and 392 bytes. That public key shapes the hardware too, as the area figures below show.

## The software record, and where it stopped

Until QUISA, QR-UOV had been evaluated only on software platforms. One earlier study implemented selected algorithms from the QR-UOV specification on modern x86 processors to explore the trade-off between signing-key compactness and signing efficiency. Another accelerated QR-UOV on x86 by working over small odd prime-power extension fields, so that hardware multipliers and SIMD vector instructions could be used.

On the hardware side there was one piece: RejSCore, a lightweight rejection-sampling architecture that pairs an AES-CTR pseudorandom generator with iterative rejection sampling, trading clock cycles for area. RejSCore shares four of its authors with QUISA, so the new design continues the group's own line of work.

The authors say, in hedged terms, that to the best of their knowledge no complete QR-UOV hardware architecture exists for FPGAs or ASICs, and in the abstract that they are the first to provide one. That is the authors' claim, and this article has not independently surveyed the literature to confirm it.

## Four gaps in prior accelerators

The paper identifies limitations in existing FPGA and ASIC work on multivariate schemes, chiefly the UOV and MAYO implementations, and QUISA is built around them.

The first is area. Existing accelerators target high performance with dedicated datapaths, such as two, three or four AES-128 instances in one MAYO design, which makes them area-intensive for resource-constrained embedded devices.

The second is a split between flexibility and parallelism. Prior designs are either coprocessors that offer flexibility at the expense of parallelism, or dedicated processors that maximise parallelism but support only one security level.

The third is scaling. Existing accelerators use dedicated datapaths per security level, so both area and clock cycles grow with the parameter set.

The fourth is the arithmetic. Existing linear system solvers use Gaussian elimination over the binary fields F2^4 and F2^8, whereas QR-UOV needs arithmetic over the prime field F127, which no existing MQ-based hardware solver supports. A binary-field solver cannot be reused by changing a parameter; the solver has to be designed for the field.

## QUISA: a unified, instruction-driven coprocessor

QUISA is an instruction-set coprocessor implemented at the register-transfer level. Its main contribution, in the authors' words, is a complete QR-UOV hardware implementation that supports instruction-driven execution of key generation, signing and verification across SL-I, SL-III and SL-V within a fixed hardware footprint.

The mechanism is simple to state. QUISA has no internal program memory. An external host issues one 82-bit instruction at a time and waits for a completion signal before issuing the next, and the decoded instruction configures a routing network between the memory units and the functional modules. Supporting all three security levels takes 431 instructions, or 4,418 bytes of host-side storage. Choosing a new salt when a signing attempt fails is a change to the instruction sequence, not to the datapath.

The unified design has a cost, and the paper is open about it. Memory is sized for the largest parameter set, SL-V, so the smaller levels run with over-provisioned memory. The authors set out how the memory would shrink for a single-level, sign-only or verify-only implementation, which is useful for anyone who needs only one of those.

All modules follow the NIST round-2 QR-UOV specification except rejection sampling, which is the next section.

## Parallel sampling: the PPSPU and the SHAKE wrapper

Pseudorandom expansion, hashing and rejection sampling run through all three QR-UOV operations, and QUISA treats them as a first-class design problem. The parallel pseudorandom sampling and packing unit, or PPSPU, runs the SHAKE-128/256 wrapper in parallel with rejection sampling to cut clock cycles.

The wrapper has two datapaths: one for the PRG and Hash operations specific to QR-UOV, which add a 2-byte initialisation vector, and one for standard FIPS 202 computations. The authors say this lets the same wrapper serve other schemes such as ML-KEM and ML-DSA without design changes or extra hardware; that is their statement, not something the paper demonstrates on those schemes.

Rejection sampling is where QUISA departs from the reference. In the round-2 reference code, rejsamp_rejection_with_aux() replaces each invalid byte in a main region with a valid byte from an auxiliary region. In hardware that needs simultaneous access to both regions, which forces irregular memory access and serialises the PRG and the sampler. QUISA instead compacts valid bytes from the main region in order and draws on the auxiliary region only when more samples are needed. The companion function, rejsamp_and_q(), is unchanged. The paper prints both versions of the modified function side by side.

The change has a verification consequence. Because it alters the reference implementation's output, it also changes the known-answer test (KAT) files, so the authors verified QUISA against modified KATs. Apart from that change, they state that QUISA remains fully compliant with the round-2 reference implementation.

The payoff is measurable. For one SL-I sampling operation (generating 2,916 pseudorandom bytes and extracting 2,808 valid ones), the earlier RejSCore design takes 8,525 clock cycles and the PPSPU takes 895, a 9.52 times speed-up in cycles.

## Small arithmetic, thanks to a Mersenne prime

To minimise area, the arithmetic unit is iterative. Modular addition uses an end-around carry: the carry bit of an 8-bit sum is folded back into the lower seven bits, which works because 2^7 is congruent to 1 modulo 127, followed by a small correction that maps 127 to zero. Multiplication of two 7-bit operands is reduced the same way.

The multipliers are small enough that DSP slices would be wasted on them, so the design builds them from look-up tables and uses no DSP blocks at all. The paper reports that the arithmetic unit, including its multiply-accumulate tile, accounts for about 5% of total LUTs. The authors note that the iterative address scheduling reduces resource use at the cost of a modest increase in clock cycles.

I'd argue this is the most transferable idea in the paper: a property of the parameter choice, a Mersenne prime modulus, flows straight into a cheap circuit. The paper makes the point for QR-UOV only, so applying it to other schemes is my suggestion, not a result.

## The F127 linear system solver

Signing in QR-UOV means solving a linear system Lx = t - u for the oil variables. QUISA's solver has three parts: an LU-decomposition block with partial pivoting, a sample solution unit that performs forward and backward substitution, and a small CopyWords unit for packing and copying data.

Two design points stand out. Because L does not depend on the random salt, the LU decomposition runs once, before the loop that retries with a new salt. And the decomposition handles rank-deficient matrices by recording the rank and pivot positions as it goes, rather than stalling or restarting. The specification puts the chance that a given salt yields no solution at no more than 1/q, about 0.8%.

The solver is the second-largest block in the design, at about 37% of LUTs. The authors suggest it could be reused by other MQ-based schemes over F127.

## What the Artix-7 results show

QUISA was written in Verilog and implemented up to post-place-and-route on an Artix-7 device (xc7a200tsbv484-3). The post-place-and-route run included KAT-based validation of key generation, signing and verification at all three security levels. Clock-cycle counts come from behavioural simulation of the full instruction sequences, and times are those counts divided by the 80 MHz clock. The paper reports no board-level measurements and no power figures.

At 80 MHz the design uses 11,144 slices, 35,248 LUTs, 18,375 flip-flops, 221 BRAM tiles and no DSPs for all three levels. The parallel sampling unit accounts for about 55% of LUTs. Memory for the public-key matrices generated during key generation takes about 80% of BRAM.

| Level | Key generation | Signing | Verification |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| SL-I | 5,330,536 cycles (66.63 ms) | 535,697 cycles (6.69 ms) | 399,777 cycles (4.99 ms) |
| SL-III | 22,594,572 cycles (282.43 ms) | 1,607,428 cycles (20.09 ms) | 1,194,832 cycles (14.93 ms) |
| SL-V | 30,415,770 cycles (380.19 ms) | 3,809,819 cycles (47.62 ms) | 2,801,059 cycles (35.01 ms) |

Key generation is the bottleneck at every level, which the authors attribute to the large public key. Verification takes 1.3 to 1.4 times fewer cycles than signing at every level. Key generation is a one-off cost per key pair; signing and verification recur.

## How it compares, with the authors' own caveats

The paper compares QUISA with other FPGA signature designs, while warning that direct comparison is hard: parameter sets differ, and the UOV and MAYO designs use AES for pseudorandom generation, whereas QUISA uses SHAKE, which costs more cycles. The authors say their goal is not to outperform existing designs but to establish the first FPGA benchmark for QR-UOV.

Against the closest relative, an FPGA implementation of UOV:

- At SL-I, QUISA uses 1.25 times more LUTs than UOV. At SL-V the trend reverses: QUISA's footprint stays near 35k LUTs while UOV's reaches 77k, so QUISA is 2.2 times more LUT-efficient there.
- QUISA uses no DSPs; UOV uses up to four.
- Key generation takes 2.20 and 1.30 times fewer cycles at SL-I and SL-V, but UOV is faster at SL-III.
- Signing and verification are slower than UOV at every level, which the authors attribute to QR-UOV's larger signatures.

Against a high-performance MAYO design that adds AES engines per level, the paper reports that MAYO reaches 6.14 times QUISA's LUTs at SL-V. Against an ML-DSA design, QUISA uses fewer LUTs (35k against 54k) but has a higher computational cost, which the authors put down to QR-UOV's large matrix operations and SHAKE-based rejection sampling. QUISA also signs faster than the cited SLH-DSA designs, which use fewer resources.

The paper also lists QUISA's cycle counts beside the round-2 AVX2 and AVX-512 software results and finds fewer cycles in every case, but it warns that FPGA and CPU cycles are architecture dependent and should not be read as an execution-time or throughput comparison.

## What a founder or engineer should take from this

First, QR-UOV now has a hardware data point. Anyone assessing the scheme for a constrained or accelerated platform had x86 figures and a lone rejection-sampling module. Now there are resource and cycle numbers at all three security levels from one design, plus a comparison with UOV.

Second, the unified footprint changes the shape of the cost question. Where earlier accelerators let area and cycles grow with the parameter set, QUISA fixes the footprint and changes the instruction stream. For a product that must support more than one security level, that is a different trade from building a datapath per level. The price is memory sized for SL-V and slower signing and verification than a dedicated UOV design.

Third, read "first" as the authors' word. They state that they are the first to provide a complete QR-UOV hardware implementation, and separately hedge that claim with "to the best of the author's knowledge".

Fourth, note the evaluation boundary. Results stop at post-place-and-route on one Artix-7 device, with cycle counts from simulation. The authors say the DSP-free design is platform-agnostic and that an ASIC version would need only its BRAMs replaced with SRAM macros; neither claim is demonstrated in the paper.

## An open question about the specification

The most interesting loose thread is the deviation from the reference. QUISA changes how rejection sampling uses the auxiliary bytes so that the generator and the sampler can run in parallel, and as a result it is verified against modified KAT files rather than the official ones.

QR-UOV is in an ongoing round-3 evaluation in 2026. Whether a hardware-motivated change to rejection sampling stays confined to one implementation, or becomes something the specification accommodates, is not something the paper addresses. It is the question I'd watch: if the scheme's hardware story is to mature alongside its security analysis, the interface between the two is where the next decision will be made.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is QUISA?**
QUISA is a QR-UOV-based instruction-set coprocessor, implemented at the register-transfer level, that runs key generation, signing and verification for SL-I, SL-III and SL-V in one design. It was developed at the Centre for Secure Information Technologies at Queen's University Belfast and published on arXiv on 21 November 2026.

**Is QUISA the first hardware implementation of QR-UOV?**
The authors say it is the first complete one. Earlier QR-UOV hardware was limited to a rejection-sampling module, and complete implementations had been reported only in software. The authors also state that, to the best of their knowledge, no complete QR-UOV architecture existed for FPGAs or ASICs.

**How fast is QUISA?**
On an Artix-7 FPGA at 80 MHz, the paper reports SL-V key generation, signing and verification in 30.4M, 3.8M and 2.8M clock cycles, or 380.19, 47.62 and 35.01 ms. At SL-I, signing takes 6.69 ms and verification 4.99 ms. The cycle counts come from behavioural simulation.

**Does QUISA follow the QR-UOV specification exactly?**
All modules follow the NIST round-2 specification except rejection sampling, which was modified so the pseudorandom generator and the sampler can run in parallel. That change alters the reference output, so QUISA was verified against modified known-answer test files.

**How does QUISA compare with hardware for UOV?**
At SL-I it uses 1.25 times more LUTs than an FPGA implementation of UOV; at SL-V it uses 2.2 times fewer. It needs no DSPs and has fewer key generation cycles at SL-I and SL-V, but signing and verification are slower at every level.
